# Developer A (Texas/Florida retail developer) - permitting is THE problem, offered to be first customer

## Bottom line

Permitting is the problem. He hammered it about 90 times and explicitly told the interviewers to drop cost, supply and materials and build only an AI permit solution. He has been evangelizing this exact idea for a while ("on my soapbox with a megaphone"). He offered to be the first customer. He is a deep, blunt, credible source and a real buyer, not a tire-kicker.

## Who he is

- President of a convenience-service retail developer: 5 to 55 acre sites, 15k-45k sqft centers anchored by mom-and-pop tenants. About 30 active projects across Texas and Florida; developed in 7 states over 4 decades; manages about 165 shopping centers. Also built five apartment projects last year.
- Connected to the interviewers through a university entrepreneurship/real-estate network and their summer program mentor-hours sessions.

## The product he wants (his words, condensed)

1. Ingest all permitting data and guidelines for a city, then the whole state.
2. Developer registers online, fills out normal info, submits plans.
3. About 20 minutes later AI has vetted the plans against every formal guideline and kicks back redlines. Fix and resubmit; within a day you are AI-passed (phase 1).
4. Phase 2: a human at the city reviews practical items not in the guidelines (curb cuts, median cuts, traffic flow). Days to weeks instead of 6-24 months.
5. Charge a fee. Separate residential vs commercial buckets to go faster.
6. Centralize all municipalities in a region so submitting to one city also flags ancillary items common across the metro. Example: one fast-growing exurban city requires 360-degree buildings (glass front and back, brick all around) while its neighbor permits a traditional center with glass on two ends and stucco. New boomtowns often have no guidelines of their own and borrow pieces from neighbors.

He noted AI would vet far beyond what a human catches and eliminates nobody's job, which kills the fear objection. He has told mayors and economic-development heads that a $100M apartment project taking 10 months to permit is 10 months of lost tax opportunity, which across a city costs hundreds of millions. Their answer: "we're looking into it."

## Why permitting and not cost/supply

- Permit timelines of 6-10 months are typical; in one case about 22-24 months and still not permitted (a Central Texas pad site for a national drive-through, coffee and retail, held up by documents that needed signatures from an adjoining school, then the city attorney, then filing of record).
- Every ripple comes back to permitting: lost city tax revenue, lender interest not accruing (a 30-month interest-only construction loan with nothing drawn; the bank suggested pulling the plug and restarting for another 24 months; refinancing would cost the bank the spread on a ~$10M loan at ~7.75-8%), equity-partner return curve flattening, tenant build-outs delayed back onto the landlord.
- "Schedule slippage / material delays" that other interviewees raised is "a code word for permits."
- On cost: "we haven't found that has been affecting us." During the lumber spike (roughly $600 to $1,800) they hedged by sequencing underground utilities and an off-site precast parking garage first, saved roughly $750-800K, and "cost is going to be cost; the volatility of the market is going to be the volatility of the market." On $60M projects it does not move the needle.

## Pain details

- Every municipality starts over from scratch; the terms are similar with small nuances. Track record does not help because it gets personal, not professional. Offices are overworked and understaffed, and many staff came from other markets with different guidelines.
- Workaround attempts: bring red pins, put the plans on the desk and ask staff to mark them up so there is no six-to-eight-month game. Still get kicked out for two or three months. Then in construction a new group of inspectors appears; one can order a drain moved and shut the job down if ignored ("an ego trap").
- Arbitrary asks not in any code: brick all the way around vs the guideline's percentage facing the main road; a $400K circular school-bus drive at a two-bedroom-max apartment project; "I don't like the color of your building" (repaint a 20,000 sqft shop pale yellow); city-council agenda slots delayed for months.
- Expediters: "window dressing, thousands of dollars, doesn't move the needle, not very effective."
- Permit fee shock: budgeted ~$150K in permits for two ~15k sqft shopping centers and two pads in a Dallas suburb; came back at about $250K per building plus $250K per out-parcel. A law firm found the city's formula, but the city said "we don't know until we issue a permit." Had they known, they would have submitted as one building and saved hundreds of thousands (they had platted four buildings to sell separately later).
- Multi-year Florida example: eight months of ping-pong with the permit department for a multi-tenant retail center with two out-parcels next to another developer's shared access, until the city admitted the developer was right.
- Old days: walk department to department, hand-carry plans to desk staff, a conference room where every division reviewed the plans together. Now you take a ticket and put it through a slot.
- Other permit layers: tenant build-out permits, sign permits, landscaping permits. Landlord tenders a building and the tenant has 160-180 days before rent starts, but the city will not issue the tenant permit until the landlord has a temporary or final certificate of occupancy (some cities demand permanent C of O because developers skipped landscaping; fix is a bond/escrow). The tenant cannot build out in time, burns money, and returns to the landlord, who eats lost rent and CAM.
- Other jurisdictions: coasts are worse (Hawaii partner reports 3-4 years for a permit; Colorado about 24 months of entitlements before the permit; Nevada and New York both painful; Florida not bad).
- Small-town example: a suburb refused three-story apartments for lack of fire equipment; he offered to buy a $250K fire truck and was refused; years later a three-story project was approved because the town realized it was losing tax revenue to a neighboring highway corridor.

## The hard part (his own caveat)

Much of the pain is people, not guidelines: inspectors flexing, arbitrary asks not in any code. AI cleanly solves the guidelines-vetting half; the personal/political half is harder. He flagged this himself. Interviewers also raised it (the school-bus example) and he agreed there is nothing in the guidelines for it.

## Business context

- Market tiers: malls (nobody builds them), power centers (100+ acres, big boxes), grocery-anchor centers, then his tier built off mom-and-pop tenants for 40 years. Larger developers feel permitting too but have 15-year horizons and are less time-sensitive.
- COVID 2020: of ~165 centers, only 6 tenants failed, 3 already on fumes, with 3 backfilled in under a month. National coffee tenants (about 25 locations in the portfolio) sent form letters asking for 25% rent cuts and were ignored because lines were out the door.
- Advice: bake permit fees into the development; the "wait and see" mantra is a trap because this may be the new norm. "Start and stop with the first biggest problem. Don't waste time on supply and cost." Going down the permit road may also open tenant, sign and landscaping permits.

## Buy signal

Strong. "You guys should investigate this." "Go down that road, see what it looks like." "If things go well with the permitting office, maybe we can help you." And at the end: "I'll be your first customer." Also invited the interviewers to his mentor hours the next Wednesday.

## Follow-ups (later notes)

- The interviewers drafted a follow-up email (not sent at the time of the note) attaching a short demo video of a one-button tool that uploads plans, vets them and moves them forward. It reported that the head of the Houston permitting office said they approve permits "at light speed," very different from his experience, and asked: when a project stalls, is it from things you did not see coming, or do you know what is needed and it still drags? The soft in-person ask for a paid pilot was to come at the next meeting.
- Two days later he emailed a link to an existing AI plan-review product (CodeComply.AI) and asked the interviewers to investigate and share their thoughts. That product is an AI building-code and plan-review platform for architects and building departments, covering IBC, IPC, IMC, NFPA 101, ADA, FHA, UFC and local amendments, claims plan-set analysis in minutes, and positions itself on reducing delays while preserving reviewer authority. It is a small (11-50 person), Miami-based company founded in 2022. The interviewers' read: it is largely the product he described unprompted, so the wedge is validated but not first; open questions are how good it is on the guidelines half, whether it touches the people/political half, who its customers are (firms vs cities), and where it is weak.
