# City Planner A (Houston Planning, Development Services) - variances, deed restrictions, why applicants fail

## Setting

Interviewer 1 and Interviewer 2 walked onto the planning floor of Houston City Hall without an appointment (the "we're university students" approach, with donuts). They spoke with three people: a land surveyor from a large engineering/survey firm waiting on a recordation appointment, one other planner/reviewer, and (main interview) City Planner A, a senior planner who handles subdivision plats and variances. He was friendly and said to email or call anytime. Walk-in access to planning is open weekdays during business hours (the permitting center is a separate location, and some city offices elsewhere in the building require appointments).

## The surveyor thread

- Waiting for a recordation appointment. His current plat took about 2 years because the prior application lapsed.
- After you get an application number you have **one year to record the plat**. Lapse causes: owner not paying property taxes, fees not paid, parties not answering emails or calls.
- The city requires a current tax certificate showing $0 owed before a property can be sold or recorded. Owners (especially on 20-acre parcels with $20k-$50k tax bills) often cannot or will not pay on time. Heavy lapses in Nov-Feb.
- His firm gets hired to clean up developer/owner messes: repay fees, resubmit already-reviewed documents, push to recordation. Land ownership is fluid; people sometimes plat before they buy to de-risk the timeline.

## Why applicants fail (the core)

- Houston has **no zoning**, so people assume they can do what they have seen elsewhere ("that lot got split into three, I'll do the same"). Wrong assumption is the number 1 trap.
- **Deed restrictions** (set by prior owners/subdivisions, can run with the land forever or auto-renew every 10 years) override what you want to build: minimum setbacks, single story, must be commercial, even paint color. Planning does not set these; they surface via the required title report.
- Other surprises that blow up timelines: floodplain drainage plan (signed and stamped by floodplain management, about 1 month), easements and pipelines you cannot build on, larger setbacks off major thoroughfares.
- Sequence of pain: applicant buys land, pays for survey and architect design, submits; the city then finds the deed restriction, setback or drainage issue. The design no longer fits, a roughly 1-month clock is running, and restarting burns fees. "Half our job is to find the mistakes."
- Results: many downscope (2 homes to 1), switch use (to a commercial use they did not want), or abandon and resell. First-timers often blame the city; he can point to the rule (the subdivision chapter of the city code).

## Variances

- If you cannot or will not meet a rule, apply for a variance. Small asks (25 ft to 20 ft setback) with good justification (walkability, pipeline easement eating half the lot, public good) can pass. Big asks (25 to 5) almost never. Pure financial justification ("I want more lots to sell") is not accepted.
- Decided by an ~18-member planning commission (developers, architects). Staff only give a recommendation; the commission can go against it either way. About 50-50. Adds time (public hearing, extra weeks).

## Who fails vs who does not

- Big firms: different problems (street/intersection spacing, parking, entrances on 200-acre jobs) but rarely rookie mistakes; dedicated staff and 15-30 years of repetition.
- Mom-and-pops and first-timers: 2-person shops, too much on their plate, "don't know what they don't know." They get crushed.

## Economics

- Delay equals money: liens accruing, loan interest. An extra week or two can be about $10k; a 2-month job slipping to 6 months adds real interest cost. Developers come in "yelling," "we've seen tears," and some try to go over staff to the director.

## Departments map

- Development Services (plats, land use, variances): "the meat and potatoes," about 12 people, the busiest group. Also Historic, Community Sustainability, GIS, and a Real Estate division for city land/leases. Permitting is a separate center. Together planning and the permitting center handle about 90% of development.

## Relevance

- Reinforces the wedge thesis: the acute, repeatable pain is on infrequent, first-time and small applicants blindsided by process knowledge (deed restrictions, title reports, drainage, setbacks, variances). Experts (consultants, expediters) already monetize navigating it. A "guarantee first-pass approval / do-it-for-you" service maps onto this population. Open question: who pays (owner, developer or consultant) and whether willingness to pay is large enough. City Planner A is a reusable inside contact for the planning/variance leg.
