# Developer A (veteran multifamily developer, AI skeptic) + Architect A on code interpretation

## Summary

Two conversations on the floor of a Houston multifamily conference. Part one is a veteran developer (self-described "dinosaur", sold his company in 2011, now a small office; buys land in cash; uses max-guaranteed-price GC contracts; knows the retail-developer mentor from the other interviews socially). Part two is an architect and a colleague at an architecture firm. Interviewer 1 and Interviewer 2 were both present;

## Developer A

- **Accuracy and trust are the real bar, not speed.** He repeatedly pushed: anyone can reduce time, but "can you increase the accuracy?" People will rely on the output; if AI errs there is liability (he cited emerging AI-liability lawsuits and new insurance products). AI tools cite sources/cases that still have to be re-checked. This matches the interviewers' read that the mentor's thing is trust.
- **Permitting confirmed as severe:** lost roughly a year in San Antonio between submittal and finalized plans (some self-inflicted). Worse since COVID because on-site systems never fully came back; a people/civil-servant problem. **Sequential reviewers:** one approves, the second flags something the first missed, and you wait 2-3 weeks to get back on a calendar. Planning is a bit easier than permitting, but planning staff sometimes say you can do something you cannot.
- **Title-report pain (adjacent opportunity):** title companies used to review backup documents for relevance; now they hand over every exception document unreviewed. He spent 5-6 hours reading ~20 linked exception documents from a 4-page report to find ~90% did not touch his property, to brief his lawyer to get them removed from the title commitment. The interviewers said they are building software to analyze codes and title reports.
- **Deal mechanics:** buys land in cash or closes right before construction (pursuit costs but no carrying loan during entitlement). Uses a max-guaranteed-price contract with the GC (overruns are the GC's risk; sometimes a shared-savings split). He speculates the retail-developer mentor's intensity about permitting reflects carrying loan interest during delays and being a controlling person who cannot control the timeline.
- **AI-market skepticism:** has heard AI mentioned more in the last three months of conferences than in all of 2025. Thinks many ex-tech founders are "throwing darts." Warns that unless a product is genuinely technical, buyers can build it themselves; cited a company that spent more on tokens than it made.
- Offered to maybe bring the interviewers into his office and to connect them to a relative (Rice master's, real estate, ex-consultant).

## Architect A (and colleague)

- Design is not their pain; code and permitting are.
- Codes change every ~3 years (Houston still on the 2021 IBC, Austin moved ahead); hard to keep current.
- Reviewer interpretation is inconsistent: they were asked for 2-hour walls that, on discussion, were not required.
- ADA / chapter 11 accessibility: enforcement varies; Houston may not truly enforce it ("just for passing"); type A vs type B units; Houston amendments carve out exceptions.
- Density (units per acre), parking ratios (per 1-bed/2-bed) and stall dimensions, and pervious vs impervious (green) area vary by city and are often unclear in the code text, forcing a lot of searching.
- The architect, not the developer, is usually the one who submits to most cities.
- Interviewer 1 described a weekend build: aggregating Houston's codes into a queryable database that answers with citations, legal precedent and how it is enforced, constantly updating. Architect's wishlist: input "200 units in Houston" and get how many must follow chapter 11 and how strictly (type A/B), plus density and parking outputs.

The interviewers are Rice sophomores in a summer innovation program working with a retail developer, with plans to attend a national architecture convention next.
