# Expediter A (small Austin permit and design shop) - code knowledge plus reviewer relationships

## The business
- 14 years expediting in Austin; no formal technical education, learned on the job.
- Started as a "glorified courier": when paperwork had to be printed and walked to the city, GCs and architects had no time to do it.
- Watching plans get rejected and architects fix them taught him the code; he became a consultant ("I looked over your plans and the city rejected for this reason, here's how to fix").
- Now permit plus design plus engineering: brokers a third-party drafter and structural engineer (middleman cut). Origin: a pool builder came with bad plans and he offered to fix them for first-time approval; this became an advertised revenue line.
- 95% residential; turns away commercial ("its own beast", deep electrical/mechanical/plumbing). Currently consulting on a 270-home job for a large homebuilder ("we are their permit monkeys").
- Client mix by volume: GCs (most of the business), architecture firms, then homeowners.

## Why submissions are kicked back
- Number one is code non-compliance with local rules, not missing documents.
- Austin traps he named: 40% lot coverage cap (site plan plus coverage calcs; 10,000 sq ft lot allows 4,000); protected trees over 19 inches (cannot dig near, must show protection); pool rules (enclosure, fencing the front yard, electrical bonding, door/window alarms); detached accessory structures (no permit under 200 sq ft, above that full code, engineered foundation and framing, lateral bracing); garage cannot face forward of the building front and if attached must be offset back about 20% of building width.
- When his own projects get kicked back it is usually because a builder went cheap and hired someone outside Austin who does not know local rules, or a homeowner bought a house with prior unpermitted work.

## Code vs relationships
- Value is both code knowledge and reviewer relationships. Reviewers like dealing with people who know the code; with his name on an application they are more inclined to move it because he back-checks his own work.
- Used to be in-person relationship greasing (candy bars); now mostly portal, email and occasional virtual meetings. Reviewers turn over, but digital relationships build through repeated dealings.
- No tricks beyond clean, well-formatted, code-correct documents.

## Knowledge is learned, not read
- The residential code and city development code are public, but knowing what to look for takes years of rejections.
- Loopholes: (1) fire-rate the garage with a 20-minute self-closing door so its area is excluded and the house stays under the 3,600 sq ft threshold that triggers a hydrant-within-600-ft-at-1,750-GPM requirement or an expensive sprinkler system; (2) Texas Chapter 245 vested rights to grandfather a lot to its platting-date entitlements, used to beat a lakefront zoning district where steep slope counted as zero buildable area. Works mainly in high-red-tape jurisdictions.

## Geography
- Austin is the breadwinner and they are slammed. A Dallas-Fort Worth expansion failed: cold calling only, no digital marketing, and DFW has little red tape so expediting is worth less. Has not tried Houston (no zoning, "weird" restrictions, no bandwidth).
- Signal: pain and willingness to pay concentrate in jurisdictions with convoluted code; the easiest market may have the least pain.

## Assessment
- Strong supply-side confirmation of a permitting thesis: the moat (jurisdiction-specific traps plus trusted relationships) is exactly what software could encode. Offered intros to a university architecture-school contact and other permit contacts. Not a buyer.
