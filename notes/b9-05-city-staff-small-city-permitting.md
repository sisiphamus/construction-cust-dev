# City Staff A, B and Chief Building Official (Texas Hill Country city) - small-city permitting

## Summary

Interviewer 2 interviewed three staff of a Texas Hill Country city of ~25,000 (City Building Staff A, City Staff B, and a Chief Building Official) on how a small city runs development and permitting and where they see AI fitting. Growth is slow and steady (1 to 1.25% a year), gated by topography and water rather than demand, and a community-driven comprehensive plan guides development. Permits move fast (7-8 day average). The staff's sharpest modern pain is a document-control gap: revisions reach the architect or applicant but not the GC, who builds off an outdated plan set. They volunteered that AI permit intake and AI plan review are next but not mature.

## Decisions

- Treated as a one-off research call. No pitch, no commitment either way.
- Open door to an in-person visit near San Antonio with a week or two of notice.

## Key points

**Water gates annexation and growth.** City is ~25,000; the county is pushing 55-60K. Growth has been 1-1.25% a year for 10-15 years. Development is driven by topography and water, not raw demand. The town sits in a river valley; running utilities over hills costs too much for buyers and the city to maintain, so they favor internal growth and redevelopment over sprawl. County groundwater rules require 10 acres per well, so developers want city density and try to annex in. The city now requires a water availability study before annexation: a developer proposing 100 acres must bring enough water credit that the existing system does not subsidize them. Council turned down a 300-400 acre annexation where proposed density outran the groundwater credit. Infill inside city limits has a right to build via zoning, and the city must serve it, so those get worked through on capacity.

**Fast permit timelines.** Most permits are approved or commented on within ~10 days; average now 7-8. Internal review used to run 10-15 days plus 3-5 days processing; now ~5 days internal with comments turned around in a day or two. The most common deficiency is insufficient information: missing energy documents, unclear site plans, or undersized/unspecified emergency egress windows (every bedroom in a one- or two-family dwelling). Usually an education gap, not a code novelty; Texas has been on the 2018 ICC codes for a long time. The pattern: people who did a lot of county work without the same oversight come into town and do not understand why the rules apply.

**Commercial code disputes and the GC-never-pulls-approved-set gap.** Commercial draws more code disputes than residential. Firewall vertical/horizontal continuity is a common miss by contractors and architects. The biggest modern headache: a denial generates revisions; the architect or applicant fixes the permit and gets it across the finish line, but nobody tells the GC doing the work. Commercial projects often have no GC at application time (a third party or the architect applies), so the GC is registered later and must add the project to their portal to see updates, which they often do not read. Meanwhile the owner handed the GC first-version plans for bidding. Since the city went digital and no longer uses paper plans on site, the GC may build from an outdated set even though code requires approved plans on site. The Chief Building Official catches this repeatedly in the field ("let me see what plans you're looking at... these are not the approved plans, you're missing the grease intercept / firewall continuation"). Their read: the discrepancy is largely caused by permits being digital; old paper markups kept everyone on one physical set.

**Comprehensive plan as backbone.** The city's long-range plan was built on heavy community engagement (town halls, resident maps of where to put parks) so the vision outlasts any single council. Council picked housing first, then downtown revitalization. Concrete outcomes: a ~130-home build where the city provided land under a development agreement to cap price points and used a tax increment reinvestment zone to hold prices down; utility master plans route improvement dollars to growth areas; the local hospital network and a private university based their strategic plans on it (including expanding nursing and tying housing to keeping doctors and nurses local). Council cites plan chapters and guiding principles on agenda items.

**Department-level survey play.** About 7-8 years ago the department surveyed stakeholders (developers, major builders, trade contractors): what are we doing well, poorly, what would "knock your socks off." They produced 15-20 items ranked by basic need vs nice-to-have and shipped most, including the digital permitting system. A year later they re-surveyed and had captured all but one item (letting inspectors change architect-signed plans in the field, which is illegal). Digital permitting allowed multi-department plan review simultaneously, cutting internal review from 10-15 days to ~5.

**"Paper felt faster" is communication style, not speed.** Some architects and developers say the old in-person process was quicker; staff think it is communication style. Older applicants struggle with a bare bullet list of deficiencies, whereas 15-20 years ago a reviewer would mark up the physical plans and show their whole thought process. Applicants who do well still book an appointment to walk through revisions in person. The office is a one-person review shop, so there is no same-day paid rapid turnaround like bigger cities. The "felt faster" complaint is largely generational: people who liked coming in, arguing, paying, leaving with a permit. When the office went online they ran classes for GCs and subs and wrote work instructions for holdouts. A large-city permit office told the interviewer the same thing: applicants pay then complain about delays, while well-run ones just check the portal and get same-day turnarounds.

**AI is what's next.** Staff volunteered that AI will affect much of the future. The big current push is AI permit software that checks submissions, nearly doing the permit-tech job of making sure all documents are there. Not there yet (they cited insurance as an industry where AI fumbles processes it has not learned). They expect to "zig and zag" as it learns. Same for AI plan review: coming, learning, not 100% there, but the clear next step.

## Main themes

- Water and topography, not demand, gate whether a small Hill Country city grows.
- A water availability study is now the deciding factor for annexation.
- Permits already move fast (7-8 days); the bottleneck is applicant education and document control, not city speed.
- Digital permitting created a document-control gap by removing the single shared physical plan set.
- Staff see AI permit intake and AI plan review as the clear next step.
