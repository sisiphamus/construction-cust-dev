# County Drainage Plan Reviewer A (large Texas county) - 3.7 submittals to approval; the office acts as educator

## Digest

**Who:** A county site-development / drainage plan reviewer with roughly 16 years in flood control, ~10 years in private drainage work, now back at the county. This is the permit-OFFICE / reviewer side, the supply side of the thesis. (Recorded call, ~33 min, auto-transcribed.)

**Why this matters (thesis validation):** Direct first-hand evidence for a first-pass-approval / AI plan-vetting bet.

- **Average 3.7 submittals before a site-development permit is approved.** Worst ~5% submit 10-15 times over months. Most are approved by the 2nd-3rd round. "If you submit 10 times they'll approve it eventually because they get tired of seeing it."
- **Rejections are for KNOWABLE, checkable reasons**, exactly what a guidelines-vetting tool could pre-catch:
    - Review-sheet info not filled out properly (the single biggest bucket). "They should know but don't, and don't put in the effort to know."
    - Wrong drainage OUTFALL assumption -> wrong detention rate. Roadside ditch 1.0 acre-ft/acre, storm sewer 0.75, flood control 0.65, state DOT has its own. Get it wrong and the whole plan is redesigned.
    - Draining to a state DOT road without a DOT permit; applicants assume it is a county street and do not research.
    - Missing plat: 14+ lots with private streets need a detention fund maintained by a government agency; applicants do not read the regulation, find out after submitting, and the project can go in the trash.
    - Incomplete packages submitted just to hit a client's 30-day deadline ("I met my role"), so the office is used as the bad guy.
- **The office is doing the applicants' QA/QC.** "Our reviews are almost like a review training... review, educate, train as we submit." Even big engineering firms hand junior EITs the work with no internal QA/QC ("go figure it out").
- **A $600 pre-development meeting prevents most of this** but is underused; those who attend are mostly "those with means," and many arrive with no engineer or architect. The county tells you up front: fire sprinklers needed, water supply (MUD vs well plus fire storage tank), left-turn lane from a traffic study (~$500k), whether the use fits. People skip it and get blindsided.

## Process detail

- County governs only DRAINAGE (floodplain/detention) and FIRE; the city handles water/wastewater and the rest. A site-development permit covers everything except the building; the fire code covers the building and cannot be approved until the site plan is. Drainage report not needed outside a floodplain unless 28+ acres.
- Site-dev permit runs through 4 county groups: commercial, public right-of-way, traffic, stormwater quality (sometimes a 5th, on-site sewage). Plus flood control district sign-off (separate agency, usually a quick sign-off unless it touches a channel; ~90% do not). Flood control was removed from the review groups when the state passed the 30-day timeframe.
- State-mandated 30-day review on first submittal (they hit ~25-30 days). Applicant turnaround varies from a week to two months; if a plat is needed applicants disappear for a month or two and some abandon.
- City vs county/ETJ: inside city limits the plan goes to the city first, then the county; in the ETJ the county approves both permit and plans. All electronic.
- Utility easements (power, pipeline) require a letter of no objection; pipelines are hard because chasing the easement owner is difficult.
- Who submits: mostly engineers, a small share expediters. Some firms are consistently easy, not necessarily bigger or smaller; "depends on the engineer at the firm, roll the dice."
- Smaller towns between Houston and Dallas/San Antonio are slower: a three-story residential complex took two years to approve; small staff, often one overloaded outsourced engineer reviewing for several cities.

## No zoning, but platting acts like it

People get excited there is no zoning, hire an architect for a full plan, then are told "you didn't even look at the plat." Newer plats restrict commercial use on residential lots; the appraisal district land-use label is not zoning.

## Cascading-requirements example (fast-food restaurant)

Want a restaurant -> needs fire sprinklers -> if on a well (not MUD water) you need a large on-site fire storage tank -> if the site was not planned for it, it does not fit -> a driveway may trigger a required ~$500k left-turn lane from a traffic study. People lay it all out, get the building permit, then find there is nowhere to put the tank. Many people buy property and propose things that cannot fit (no room for detention).

## His recommended ideal flow

Engineering site assessment (~$1,500, a 1-2 page write-up of jurisdiction, water/wastewater, outfall, floodplain) -> architect schematic sketch (~a couple thousand) -> $600 pre-development meeting with county and city planning BEFORE drawing the full (expensive) plan set.
