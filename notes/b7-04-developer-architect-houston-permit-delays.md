# Developer A (Houston multifamily developer) + Architect A (its exclusive architecture firm) - Houston permitting delays, reviewer inconsistency, code interpretation

## Who

- **Developer A** - partner / SVP of multifamily development at a Houston multifamily developer-operator. Public conference speaker. Connected to the interviewers through the company's president via a university summer venture program.
- **Architect A** - partner at the Houston architecture firm that is the only one the developer has ever used. Deep in design and entitlements; sits on an ICC/IBC code committee. A second partner of that firm (Architect B) joined partway through.
- Interviewers 1 and 2 led the call as customer development on permitting.

## Summary

The group agreed the number one holdup is review time, not documents. Houston quotes about 13 days but real timelines run 20-35 calendar days per round, with 3-4 submittals, so 5-6 months total is normal. Houston is decent compared to Las Vegas (6 months for comments, 2 years for a building permit), Austin (site permit 12-18 months; site development permit touches ~80 reviewing departments), California and Boulder. The deeper pains are throw-down comments, reviewer inconsistency across rounds, staff turnover, and code interpretation.

## Key points

**Where it breaks (Houston specifics)**
- Houston says 90% of cases clear in about 13 days; reality is 20-35 calendar days per round. Planning has a 30-day legal max in Texas, but unlike plats (auto-approved if the city misses 30 days) there is no auto-approval penalty for permits, so the deadline has no teeth.
- Slowest departments on big projects: storm drainage and traffic (storm is run by public works, slower than building departments). One current project is stuck on electrical and structural.
- Multifamily-specific: the site development plan (site plan and life-safety review with the fire marshal) was outside the city's digital portal until about a year ago (the architect lobbied for ten years). Planning always puts the newest, least experienced planner on it; one review came back with 8 of 10 comments that did not apply.

**Why deals are fragile (developer stakes)**
- Developers do not land-bank. They line up land purchase to close simultaneously with the new-construction loan. If the city drags, they can lose the land (seller won't extend) after already spending heavily on architect and consultants. "The whole thing could fall apart if you get some nut at the city who decided to take their family leave and not come back."
- Late permits push the schedule and strain the developer-architect relationship; the architect builds estimated permit times into the schedule and gets blamed when the city misses.

**Throw-down comments (architect's number 1 complaint)**
- On first submission about half the comments are throw-downs (generic, already answered in the drawings), half are real. A mayoral mandate to push comments out in 30 days made it worse: if a reviewer hasn't reviewed in 30 days, they dump throw-down comments and send it out, so big projects get half-reviewed or unreviewed.

**Reviewer inconsistency (number 2)**
- You don't keep the same reviewer between rounds. Resubmit after addressing round-1 comments and a new reviewer adds net-new comments that should have surfaced earlier. "Why do I have four new comments? Where did those come from?"
- Wanted fix: guarantee that once you get a reviewer, you stay with them. Houston now offers roughly twice-weekly scheduled meetings with department reviewers and paid overnight/overtime reviews (~$500 per department), but the overtime reviewer often isn't the one who knows the comments.
- Reviewer assignment is a lottery ("next up gets the job"), though certain reviewers tend to get certain product types.

**Turnover**
- Good reviewers get promoted into management or poached by private firms; the people who can review plumbing go work for plumbing companies. "The bottom-of-the-barrel guys end up sticking around." Most reviewers the architect knew have retired in the last five years. Old relationship leverage (hand-walking plans between departments, knowing a supervisor 20-25 years) is fading in the digital era.

**Code interpretation (the architect's core thesis)**
- "The problem with code is interpretation." Three people read the same code three ways; on his ICC/IBC committee 15 people each have a different opinion on the same question. Multifamily is one of the most nuanced products a city handles.
- Third-party reviewers (hired by small towns lacking in-house expertise) have misaligned incentives: to look thorough they pile on comments and the city won't override them. Example: a third-party reviewer told a client a 5-story wood-frame multifamily could not be built under the code in a Colorado suburb; the architect spent 90 minutes walking the reviewer through the code, got it cleared, and four more were built there after.

**AI for code compliance (unprompted from the architect)**
- He uses Claude, Grok and others for code search. His read: about 70% accurate, "a lot wrong." Failure mode: local amendments. AI reads the base code and misses jurisdiction-specific amendments; you must tell it exactly which code and amendments to use.
- More accurate when you upload the specific code and amendments and tell it what to check, versus uploading a drawing and asking "is this compliant?" He said gathering that information is the architect's job.
- Fear: building officials using AI to generate comments and pass them on unreviewed. He can already tell when third-party QC checks are AI-generated.
- He is researching how to deploy AI across his firm. An AI vendor said they pull code "off the website," which he laughed at because city websites are stale. Real example: a Texas county changed its fire code (2018 to 2021 IBC, sprinkler requirement), posted it only on social media (not the website or office), and about six projects absorbed seven-figure change orders. You have to call and get the current code in writing from the building official.

**Reaction to the interviewers' idea (tracking reviewer and code decisions)**
- The firm already does a manual version: walking into a new city with 60-100 prep questions to lock interpretations before designing. Architect A is "intrigued" but flags scale: it is tens to hundreds of thousands of decisions, not hundreds, so only worth it as an AI-searchable database, which requires the city to invest. Houston has "code words" (one-page write-ups of major past interpretations, ~20 years' worth) and ICC publishes hundreds of formal interpretation letters, so the precedent format exists but is not searchable at scale. Tracking reviewers across projects only pays off if you submit 10-20+ projects per year, not one.

**Forecasting**
- They forecast permit timelines from historical experience plus a phone call to the city right before submitting ("what's your review period right now?"), since timelines swing with staffing. They don't survey other firms; they ask civil engineers, consultants and their expeditor (who talks to other expeditors).

**Other process friction**
- Houston's digital portal had a catastrophic launch about five years ago (the architecture firm and two other multifamily firms uploaded 10 projects of 500-1000 sheets each and crashed it to prove it lacked capacity). Much better now, with a decent pre-checklist. But only one person can submit per project, forcing workarounds (shared expeditor login), and each reviewer wants drawings filed in different folders and issues a comment instead of moving them.
- Phase permitting (separate site, foundation, garage and building permits so a client can close a loan on a site permit) multiplies tracking; the developer mostly avoids it.

**Faster jurisdictions to copy**
- Dallas: one submittal, then a live redline meeting with all licensed consultants, marked up and stamped on the spot in 1-2 days; worth flying consultants in and a ~$60k city fee to save ~3 months. Austin has an Express version done live on Zoom with 24 hours to upload updated drawings. A small Houston-area city returns reviews in 10 business days and sticks to it.

**Market context**
- Down cycle: fewer projects, cities downsize, capacity swings. Deals don't pencil: projects built 4-5 years ago appraise below replacement cost; operating costs (taxes doubled, insurance and payroll way up) outran rent growth (~3%); 5-year mini-perms refinance into much higher rates. Most building is on other people's money with minimal equity. The architecture firm is going back to basics: simpler floor plans, fewer options, cheaper buildings. A recent project ran about $50M soft plus hard cost versus about $35M ten years ago.
