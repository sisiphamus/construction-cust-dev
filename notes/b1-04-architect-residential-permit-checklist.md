# Architect B (3-person Houston residential firm) - mental checklist for city submissions

## Context
Interviewer 2 had visited the Houston permitting office and talked to cities between Houston and Dallas about larger commercial projects, and wanted the residential/remodel/addition view.

## Permitting
- The city follows the international building and residential codes; the code book is too thick for reviewers to check everything, so they look for certain items. Architects learn by trial and error what must be on the plan (stair details, smoke detectors, etc.).
- Her workaround: before submitting, review the drawings "with the city in mind". About 10-15 things in total; "it would be nice if they could share their checklist." Surprises still happen with conditions never seen before.
- Historic districts add a review by a historic committee.
- Deed restrictions apply to residential neighborhoods (rarely commercial); understanding each neighborhood's rules during design can be harder than city review. Interviewer 2 said the planning office told him builders most often hit problems with deed issues.
- Quote: "I definitely have horror stories... we expect you to be perfect, but if we make a mistake, nobody pays for it. But you have to suffer through working with us even though it was our mistake."

## Practice
- Three partners share design, permitting, client management, site visits, and pricing/bid negotiation with builders. Schematic design is about visualization; construction documents are technical; different audiences (homeowner, builder, city).
- Always start with three design options so clients compare and explore early; few changes during construction as a result.
- 3D renderings in SketchUp and increasingly Revit so couples see the same thing; some people cannot visualize from 2D plans.
- Repeat-builder relationships; for each client she recommends about three builders matched on personality, price and quality, and gives clients questions to ask references. One bad match in her career, when the client chose the lowest bidder.
- Residential is emotionally heavier than commercial, where "everybody goes home and their job is over."
