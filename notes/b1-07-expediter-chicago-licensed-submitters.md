# Expediter B (Chicago expediting service) - only two entities can submit

## Context
Interviewer 2 was mapping how other markets run permitting to help a Houston developer that does about six projects a year (a couple of single-family homes, strip malls, three-story apartments) and gets held up for months to years on permits. Speaker labels in the source were unreliable; attribution to the expediter is inferred from content.

## Chicago structure
- Anything needing architectural review must be submitted by either a licensed expediter or the architect who drew the plans. A lawyer, contractor or owner cannot submit. This creates accountability (someone owns the submission) but concentrates work in few hands.
- Biggest holdup: only so many architects and reviewers, plus zoning. If the use does not match zoning, the case goes to the zoning review board.
- Electrical and small permits can be filed and cleared in a day or two; architectural review is the slow part.
- Larger jobs need a GC with all trades licensed; the owner works with GC, architect, and a licensed expediter.
- Emerging team model: overseas drafters partnered with a US architecture firm that provides the stamp, with the expediter reviewing before it goes to the city.
- Across Illinois, many towns outsource building inspections to freelance certified inspectors; the department exists on paper.

## Ideal process
- Assemble the team (architect, expediter, feasibility/survey firm) before or right after buying land to see what can actually be built. Check zoning map, plan of survey, and codes first so you do not buy and then give up.
- Hidden costs such as turn lanes or required well connections can kill a deal.
- The full team approach pencils only for multimillion-dollar projects, not $50k ones.
