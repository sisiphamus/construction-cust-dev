# Architect A (founder of a schematic design software startup) - adoption of opinionated AEC software is the struggle

## Summary

Architect A, a long-time licensed architect, built a software tool that automates schematic design (what can be built on a plot) for architects and real estate developers working on large, complex multifamily projects. The software is highly opinionated, built from decades of her and her colleagues' experience, which makes it powerful but hard to onboard. The company grew entirely organically: one day they found they had been ranked a top AI tool for architects without ever investing in marketing. The call was mostly Interviewer 1 sharing observations from a national architecture convention, with her asking questions and sharing what she is working on.

## Key points

- **Product:** a separate platform (not a plug-in), exports to Revit; used for planning the building at the schematic stage. Currently multifamily (founder's passion and experience, belief that the US is missing ~1.5M housing units and multifamily is the most sustainable form; single-family is not of interest); next version to add hospitality and educational. "You need to be a smart user," but it automates much of the tedious work in that stage.
- **Team:** in-house designers, software developers, algorithm/AI experts and QA. Domain experts teach the math/AI people about design and planning; the work is analyzing the process and turning it into mathematics and algorithms. A couple of years in. Uses its own algorithms rather than LLM-based data-center-heavy models for the product; uses LLMs internally for writing.
- **Marketing:** none. Organic growth she calls "a miracle."
- **Biggest pain: adoption.** "It's a struggle every day. Very difficult. You need to educate users." They run training courses and onboarding and are "always struggling."
  - Hardest part is changing the flow users are used to and getting them to adopt a different way of thinking.
  - "Very opinionated software": users have to understand how the software thinks and adapt to it; assumptions inside only make sense to someone who understands the market and planning challenges. "You can't just jump on it, get value."
  - Many people now just want to prompt and get something; that does not work on complex professional projects.
  - Once users learn it, they save a lot of time and get a lot of value.
- **Robotics:** she is enthusiastic. Interviewer 1 described a construction-layout robot that marks floors with tape and measurements from architect plans before walls go up (robots work best in unconstrained environments), and a Scandinavian example of robots drilling holes overnight. She thinks robotics adoption on site may not be harder than design software because pain is large (worker shortage, safety).
- **Industry observations from Interviewer 1 (national architecture convention):**
  - Compliance tools were the most visible category (Itchy, OpenCodes, CodeComply: upload plans, flag issues against municipality codes), plus materials estimating and schedule optimization.
  - Data center and mega-project boom: 2025 spend of $60B+ on projects over $1B each; workers need nearby residential; boom forecast to taper off ~2030. A chief economist said 20-24 year olds are not entering blue collar trades fast enough, which is the opportunity for robotics.
  - AI attitudes were polarized, no middle ground: "using AI and wanting more" versus "see the promise but haven't taken the first step." About 50% of sessions had "AI" in the title. Architects were more open than contractors and developers, who mostly do not want to be near it. FOMO plus fear of not knowing where to start; adoption is the bottleneck.
  - Environmental objections to AI (data center water and electricity use) were raised by some attendees.
- She also pointed to a consulting-firm report on construction industry productivity (2017, updated 2024) as the best map of industry problems.
