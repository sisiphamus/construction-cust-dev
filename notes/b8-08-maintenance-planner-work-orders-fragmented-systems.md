# Maintenance Planner A (federal site operations contractor) - planning 80+ work items across disconnected systems

## What the role looks like

- He is planner, scheduler and first-line supervisor in one. The schedule is agreed across multiple organizations and runs about two months out; as the execution week gets close everything tightens and everything committed that week has to get done.
- Example: he had to confirm equipment (a man-lift and two exhaust fans) and filters were on site before locking the week in; if equipment is not available he schedules rental at risk.
- Two-week schedule held 81 items at once; about 55 items on the docket at a time, some not due for 6 months. For each he has to ensure materials, people and resources.
- Work orders come from the maintenance system automatically (for example, a preventive maintenance done six months ago generates a new work order). He must verify the equipment and conditions still match, since some work orders are no longer needed, some are weekly and some date to five years ago. Corrective maintenance appears when someone reports a problem (a pump making a weird noise) or maintenance finds something broken.
- Morning operations meeting each day; a mid-afternoon check-in on what was done and what is planned. Operators run the plant (steam, process water, fire water) and maintenance fixes it; if fire water is down an operator must walk the site every two hours.
- Lockout / hazardous-energy procedures: each job needs a hazard analysis and lockout sheet; larger jobs need larger lockout processes depending on the work, location (near power lines needs a barrier; over a building requires making sure no one is inside). Some requirements are "tribal knowledge," such as which two jobs must be done together.
- A simple job can take four months to plan, and in radiological facilities requires many inspections and organizations. Simple jobs become very difficult in some places (some fans cannot have any downtime). Site walk-downs check for conflicts such as parked vehicles blocking the lift.
- Equipment rentals go through a separate site organization that owns forklifts, man-lifts and other powered equipment; billing starts when equipment leaves the yard and stops when it returns, with each requester allotted a yearly operating budget. He plans equipment months ahead.
- Wage-law limits (Davis-Bacon): work per work order is capped; the hours allowed per order dropped from about 90 to 40, with a dollar limit of roughly $2,000 per work order. Exceeding it requires approval from the wage-law administrator and often hands the job to construction or a subcontractor (rare).
- Multiple facility managers handle different areas of the site.

## Pain: fragmented systems

- Work is tracked in multiple databases: one for preventive maintenance, an asset-management system with plant information, plus separate programs for deferrals and parts.
- "There should be one database that does it all." He must file a preventive-maintenance deferral in one program, then record that it has been deferred in a different program; drawings and equipment should be entered once and flow into work-request generation.
- He acknowledges a single all-in-one system is a big lift and a single point of failure ("all your eggs in one basket").
- Parts: he enters needs in the software but must separately order belts, filters and other items and confirm stock; a separate person handles receiving and keeps up with part-number changes.
