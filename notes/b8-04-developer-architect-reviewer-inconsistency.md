# Developer B (Houston multifamily developer) + Architect A (Houston architecture firm partner) - permitting delays, reviewer inconsistency, code interpretation

## Who

- **Developer B:** partner / SVP of multifamily development at a Houston multifamily developer and operator. Public speaker at industry events. Connected to the interviewers through the retail developer president who complained about permitting (see another note) and the university summer program.
- **Architect A:** partner at the Houston architecture firm that is the only one the developer has ever used. Deep in design and entitlements; sits on an ICC/IBC code committee.
- **Architect B:** Architect A's partner, joined partway through.
- Interviewers led the call as customer development on permitting.

## Summary

Both agreed the number one holdup is review time, not documents. Houston quotes about 13 days (said to clear ~90% of cases) but real timelines run 20-35 calendar days per round with 3-4 submittals, so 5-6 months total is normal. Houston is decent compared to Las Vegas (6 months for comments, 2 years for a building permit), Austin (site permit 12-18 months; the site development permit touches ~80 reviewing departments), California and Boulder.

## Key points

**Where it breaks (Houston)**
- Planning has a 30-day legal maximum in Texas, but unlike plats (auto-approved if the city misses 30 days) there is no auto-approval penalty for permits, so the deadline has no teeth.
- Slowest departments on big projects: storm and traffic (storm is run by public works and is slower than building departments). One current project was stuck on electrical and structural.
- Multifamily-specific: the site development plan (site plan / life-safety review with the fire marshal) was outside the digital portal until about a year ago, after about 10 years of the architect asking. The planning department assigns the newest, least experienced planner; one review returned 8 of 10 comments that did not apply.

**Why deals are fragile (developer stakes)**
- Developers do not like to land-bank. They line up land purchase to close simultaneously with the new-construction loan. If the city drags, they can lose the land (seller will not extend) after already spending heavily on architect and consultants. "The whole thing could fall apart if you get some nut at the city who decided to take their family leave and not come back to work for a long time."
- Late permits push the schedule and strain developer relationships; the architect builds estimated permit times into the schedule and gets blamed when the city misses.

**Throw-down comments (architect's number one complaint)**
- On the first submission about half the comments are "throw-downs" (generic, already answered in the drawings), half are real. A mayoral mandate to push comments out in 30 days made it worse: if a reviewer has not reviewed in 30 days they dump throw-down comments and send it out, so big projects get half-reviewed or unreviewed.

**Reviewer inconsistency (number two)**
- You do not keep the same reviewer between rounds. By the third submission a different reviewer adds net-new comments that should have surfaced earlier. "Why do I have four new comments? Where did those come from?"
- New reviewers do not re-raise cleared throw-downs, but bring fresh ones.
- Wanted fix: "If the city could guarantee that once I get my reviewer, I stay with the reviewer, that would reduce the whole review out of our process." Houston now offers about twice-weekly scheduled meetings with department reviewers, and paid overnight/overtime review (about $500 per department), but the overtime reviewer often is not the one who knows the comments.
- Reviewer assignment is "next up gets the job," though certain reviewers tend to get certain product types.

**Turnover**
- Good reviewers get promoted to management or poached by private firms (plumbing reviewers go work for plumbing companies). Interviewer 1's framing: "the bottom-of-the-barrel guys end up sticking around." Most reviewers the architect knew have retired in the last five years. The old relationship leverage (hand-walking plans department to department) is fading in the digital era; it is now "more about how you process it."

**Code interpretation (architect's core thesis)**
- "The problem with code is interpretation. I can have three people read the code and interpret it differently." Multifamily is one of the most nuanced products a city handles.
- Third-party reviewers hired by small towns lacking expertise are a recurring nightmare: to look thorough they pile on comments and the city will not override them. Example: a third-party reviewer told a client "you cannot build this project under this code" on a 5-story wood-frame multifamily in a Colorado suburb; the architect spent 90 minutes walking the reviewer through the code (while standing in an identical 5-story wood-frame build in Denver) and got it cleared. Four more were built there afterward.

**AI for code compliance (unprompted from the architect)**
- Uses general LLM assistants for code search. His read: about 70% accurate, "a lot wrong." Failure mode is local amendments: AI reads the base code and misses jurisdiction-specific amendments.
- More accurate when you upload the specific code plus amendments and tell it what to check, rather than uploading a drawing and asking "is this compliant?" Gathering that information is "our job as the architect."
- Even with the right code, interpretation remains. His fear: building officials use AI to generate comments and pass them along unreviewed; he can already tell when third-party QC checks are AI-generated.
- He is researching how to deploy AI across his firm. An AI vendor told him they pull code "off the website," which he laughed at because city websites are stale. Real example: a Texas county changed its fire code (2018 to 2021 IBC, with a sprinkler requirement), posted it only on social media (not the website or office door), and about 6 projects absorbed seven-figure change orders. You have to call and get the current code in writing from the building official.

**Reaction to the interviewers' pitch (tracking reviewer/code decisions)**
- Interviewer 1 proposed tracking past reviewer interpretations and other firms' experiences to pre-empt comments and forecast. The architecture firm already does a version internally: entering a new city with 60-100 prep questions to lock interpretations before designing.
- Architect A is "intrigued" but flags scale: it is not hundreds of decisions but tens to hundreds of thousands; only worth it as an AI-searchable database, which requires the city to invest. Houston has "code words" (one-page write-ups of major past interpretations, 20 years' worth) and ICC publishes hundreds of formal interpretation letters, so the precedent format exists but is not searchable at scale. Tracking reviewers across projects is only worth the manpower if you submit 10-20+ projects a year (as this developer does), not one a year.

**Forecasting**
- They forecast permit timelines from historical experience plus a phone call to the city right before submitting ("what's your review period right now?"), because timelines swing with city staffing. They do not survey other architecture firms; they ask civil engineers, consultants and their expeditor, who talks to other expeditors.

**Other process friction**
- Houston's digital permit portal had a catastrophic launch about five years ago (the firm and two others uploaded 10 projects of 500-1,000 sheets each and crashed the system to prove it lacked capacity). Much better now, with a decent pre-checklist. But only one person can submit per project, forcing workarounds (a shared expeditor login). Reviewers each want drawings in different folders and issue a comment to move them rather than fixing it themselves.
- Phase permitting (separate site, foundation, garage and building permits so a client can close a loan on a site permit) multiplies tracking into many parallel sets. The developer mostly avoids it; much of its new construction is on HUD debt.

**Faster jurisdictions (the model to copy)**
- Dallas: one submittal, then a live redline meeting where all licensed consultants attend, mark up and stamp on the spot, done in 1-2 days. Worth flying consultants in and paying a roughly $60K city fee to save about 3 months. "If you get your permit in two months versus six months, you would pay to fly all your consultants up there." Austin has an Express version done live over video with 24 hours to upload updated drawings. A small Houston-area city returns in 10 business days and sticks to it.

**Market context**
- Down cycle: fewer projects, cities downsize and lay off staff, capacity swings.
- Deals do not pencil: projects built 4-5 years ago appraise below replacement cost; operating costs (taxes doubled, insurance and payroll way up) outran roughly 3% rent growth; 5-year mini-perms refinance into much higher rates and go underwater. Most building is on other people's money with minimal equity, which is the structural fragility. The architecture firm is going "back to basics": simpler floor plans, fewer options, cheaper buildings. A recent developer project ran about $50M soft plus hard cost vs about $35M ten years ago.

## Main themes

- Bottleneck is review time and process, not document completeness; Houston is mid-pack good.
- Process pain is human: throw-down comments, losing your reviewer, lottery assignment.
- Turnover hollows out review quality; least experienced reviewers get the hardest product (multifamily).
- Code interpretation is the deep problem, not code lookup.
- AI is real and in use but about 70% accurate, failing on local amendments and stale published codes.
- Faster jurisdictions show the fix: one live redline-and-stamp meeting; developers will pay to save months.

## Quotes

- "I submit the first time, they give me the same comments every single time... half the comments you get in the first submission are throw-downs... Other half are real. You go two submissions in, you get to the third submission, and you don't get the same reviewer. And somebody else, they add new comments."
- "AI is probably 70% accurate... multifamily can be very, very nuanced if you don't know all the amendments to the location you're building in."
