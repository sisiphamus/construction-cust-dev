# Vendor Rep A and colleagues (security/access-control vendor) - fire marshal inconsistency, AI as a tool

## Summary

A noisy multi-party conversation on the floor of a Houston multifamily conference with staff from a national security and access-control manufacturer (access control, video surveillance, visitor management; they also acquired a residential-tour software company) and a regional monitoring/installation company. Speaker attribution is best-effort.

## Decisions

None.

## Actions

- Exchange LinkedIn connections with the vendor/installer people. No commitment.

## Key takeaways

- **Fire marshal is the real, discretionary gate on fire/life-safety.** "The fire marshal basically has the last say." The standard exists but is loosely worded, so marshals fill gaps with personal preference, and the same marshal may rule differently across nearby jurisdictions (one Houston-area suburb versus the next) "because it feels like it." Roughly 30-40% of projects need redesign to satisfy the marshal; without sign-off nothing moves. It is not extremely frequent per rule but happens often enough to matter.
- **Counties and municipalities are slow with no incentive to speed up** ("they get to it whenever they want to get to it").
- **Online permitting maturity varies by city.** College Station has a fully online system; a rep with a roofing/construction background could pull a permit remotely there. Houston has tried to go fully online but government adopts tech far more slowly than even the private-sector "old processes" that panelists complained about. Opinion voiced: the Houston permitting office needs an AI processing workflow.
- **AI-as-tool framing (strong, repeated):** "AI" is not a product; a real business solves a specific problem (for example, makes a piece of paper or documentation more efficient) and uses AI to do it. Adoption burden objection: "I now need to get my entire workforce to adopt your AI." Mindset: don't build for the current model, build for what exists a year from now. Distinction between using AI (making a grocery list) and utilizing it (behavior analysis, face matching). One vendor has used machine learning for about ten years and is expanding into software.
- **Interviewers' pitch:** their startup began as permitting help and evolved into a closed-loop AI operating system that tracks every interaction and learns from outcomes; the value is in what it learns, not the output. Discussion that cold outreach is a moving target: recipients now distrust over-polished AI-written emails and prefer authentic-looking ones.
- **Origin of the permitting focus:** the interviewers were referred to a Houston multifamily developer's president through a university entrepreneurship program; he said "permitting" about 100 times in an hour as his single greatest pain.
- Rest of the conversation was conference banter (household robots, AI doom jokes) with no customer-development value.
