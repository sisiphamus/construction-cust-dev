# City Plumbing Chief Inspector A (large Texas city permitting center) - plumbing plan review and inspection, how it really works

## Why this matters

The interviewers are exploring permitting software that vets plans against jurisdiction rules. This contact is a division manager (chief plumbing inspector), a direct line into how plan review and field inspection work and why submissions fail. Treat as primary-source domain knowledge. The contact gave a business card and offered to take follow-up questions by email (a warm direct contact inside the plumbing division). A plan analyst supervisor was mentioned briefly. Buy signal: none (research conversation).

## Key takeaways

**Why jobs fail (the #1 thing):** Not installing per the approved plans. Two recurring causes:

- Field value-engineering: contractors swap a spec'd material for a cheaper or preferred one that is still code-approved (classic example: engineer specs copper water piping, plumber installs PEX because it is cheaper, faster and comes in a roll). It looks fine but is not what the approved plans called for, so it gets rejected.
- Bidding off the wrong drawings: plumbers bid off the construction set, not the approved set, so they are unaware the other material was spec'd. Any deletion of fixtures or change of material requires resubmitting plans for a new approval. They do it anyway because resubmitting causes delays; the inspector says they never get a good answer for why contractors skip it.

**Codes and the update cycle:** Each code body publishes every ~3 years. The city is on the 2021 code; the bodies already have 2024 and 2027 out, so the city is behind and not moving to adopt soon. It went 2012 -> 2015 (held the 2012 for years), then 2015 -> 2021 in about a year. No city keeps up with every cycle.

- Two code bodies: ICC (I-codes, used by Dallas) and IAPMO (Uniform Plumbing/Mechanical Code, used by this city and likely Austin). ICC publishes nearly everything except electrical (National Electrical Code).
- Interpretation varies by region: soil (Houston digs easily; Austin/San Antonio hit caliche/rock), frost zones (water pipe min 12 inches deep in Houston, deeper in Dallas), and floodplain (in a known flood zone, certain appliances, gas meters and backflow preventers must be elevated above floodplain).
- Code is a legal document: "shall" means must, "may" means it is discussable. Many code-compliant ways exist to build the same system as long as it meets the intent and spirit of the law.
- Engineered systems: the code allows alternate methods/materials when it does not specifically address something. The engineer supplies data, a separate group reviews it, and the building official signs off. Some jobs (e.g. a non-standard request in an existing mall) require the chief to inspect personally.

**Staffing and throughput:** Full staff would be 44 (permit techs, senior field inspectors, one assistant, inspectors). Chronically understaffed because city wages cannot match private (a union plumber vs city inspector can be ~$10/hr apart; private hours are longer, 60-70/week plus on-call rotations, vs 40). A recent new hire left after 4-5 months back to private. Draws for the job: stability, paid holidays and leave, benefits. Hiring is via postings, social media and word of mouth from inspectors to contractors ("the million dollar question"). Inspectors are in the field 90%+ of the time, averaging ~22-23 inspections a day (peaked at 30 with overtime); the target for a quality inspection is 15. Pre-COVID the office ran 650-700/day, better staffed. After a long weekend or system outage, backlog doubles (about 1,000 vs 400-500 a day). All inspections are cleared same-day. ~70% of inspectors are certified, but that does not equal what they know. Monthly group meeting covers code items.

**How plumbers actually learn code (red-tag education):** Most field plumbers do not own or read the code book. When a job fails, the inspector leaves a red tag listing deficiencies; that is how the plumber learns for next time. Inspectors get name-dropped ("the chief said I could do this") to pressure less experienced inspectors into passing. Quote: "I don't want to know what you think or feel, I want to know what you know." It takes 2-5 years to make a good inspector; since inspectors now work from separate zones, learning from peers is slower (they use a group text).

**Inspector consistency:** Variation in leniency is about personality, not experience. "Labrador" types want to be liked and pass things they maybe should not; "new sheriff" types over-enforce on personal preference. Neither is right; the book is the standard. Hardest part of the job: argumentative contractors/customers who do not want to follow standards.

**Plumber training path (context):** Union apprenticeship is ~5 years of school plus work, mostly new construction; non-union requires ~8,000 hours before the journeyman exam. Inspectors need plumbing theory (sizing water, gas, drains, venting; how one connection affects another), not just install practice.

**Why the rules exist (life safety):** Cross-connection/backflow can contaminate potable water (open garden hose in a fertilized puddle siphoned back during a water-main break; a kinked toilet fill tube with a pinhole drawing blue tablet water into an ice maker; an improperly installed water softener in El Paso pulled sewer water into the drinking supply and forced a boil notice for a whole area).

**Tech/process changes:** 30 years ago everything was pen and paper. Over ~20 years: Palm Pilots -> phones -> tablets. Paper plans -> all-digital plan submittals; inspectors and the office both have field access (previously huge plan rolls had to be brought in). Inspection results sync electronically. A recent mayoral initiative targets residential plans out in 30 days. Residential plans get lighter review (mostly architectural soundness) so the inspector must field-verify sizing, because residential drawings do not spell out pipe sizes like commercial ones do.

**Complexity by job type:** Small (lease spaces, outdoor kitchens, residential adds, pools) is simple. Large (medical center, high/mid-rise, hospitals) gets complicated: engineers miss overlays (plumbing runs into mechanical), beams not accounted for, and fast deadlines push contractors to request partial releases so they can occupy and get a draw ("it's always about the money"). Apartments are less complex. Medical gas systems need a separate state endorsement and follow a different code. Inspection sequence on a new build (e.g. restaurant): groundwork (all plumbing inside the building footprint, grease vs waste lines kept separate until the grease trap, trap primers on floor drains, grease trap type per drawings) -> rough-in (in-wall: drainage, waste, venting, traps, fire caulking at penetrations) -> finals. Inspectors pull the job on their phone, confirm address and scope, and form an action plan; gas service jobs start with a pressure test.

**Plan reviewers vs inspectors:** The city uses specialized reviewers per trade. The chief inspector is the go-to liaison for plan review and occupancy (occupancy = a separate group inspecting already-occupied commercial sites for unpermitted alterations; every business needs a certificate of occupancy). Questions flow both directions to keep uniformity.

**Expediters / permit runners:** Some work closely with the office, especially engineers trying to pass plans the first time, though plan review deals with them more than inspections. Others act as permit runners / middlemen chasing finals for contractors.

**Integrity:** Bribery is rare but real (a handful of times in 30+ years, including one recorded solicitation this year). Random drug tests; refusal or a positive means termination.

**Where to learn more:** The city permitting center website has the start-to-finish permit/submittal process and required documents (e.g. what is needed to install a water meter). Both the ICC I-codes and the IAPMO UPC are online read-only, plus local amendments. There is not much beyond that for inspections; every city differs procedurally but shares a base code plus municipal amendments (which the city has tried to minimize).
