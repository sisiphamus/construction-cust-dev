# Mechanical Sub Owner A (NJ HVAC/electric/plumbing sub) - cash flow, materials, labor pains

## Digest

**Who:** Owner of a non-union metro NY/NJ mechanical subcontractor running three trades (HVAC, electric, plumbing). Backlog roughly $150-250M (about two years of work), about $10-15M per month of work, tens of millions in receivables outstanding at any time. About 50/50 commercial new construction versus residential service. Single-channel auto-transcript; expect transcription errors.

**Ranked pains (his words):**

1. **Payment / cash flow is the number 1 hindrance.** Subs are "the bottom of the heap," front $80k+ on mobilization and wait 45-60 days (some over 90) to get paid. Roughly $25-35M is owed to him at any moment. Financing across the industry is tightening, developers hold money longer, insurance and medical costs are way up, and costs are up 60-100% over six years. Payment runs on AIA pay applications: the GC redlines the draw, then owner and bank approval; 45-60 days out. Slow pay then trickles down: his equipment vendor wants payment on time or applies a premium.
2. **Material and supply cost volatility plus tariffs.** Prices up sharply since COVID; tariffs used as an "excuse" by manufacturers; no pricing transparency (you can only vet by checking other manufacturers; manufacturers split the country, even NJ, into pricing regions). Fixed-price contracts bid about 10 months ago are now underwater; renegotiation mostly fails ("show me when prices ever came down"; savings are never shared when prices drop, except maybe in large trades like steel erection). Copper, transformers, generators and switchgear are up with data-center demand. Bonds (bid, performance, payment) can cost several hundred thousand dollars on a large job and are often not markupable; a weaker company pays a higher bond rate.
3. **Skilled labor.** Find, train, keep: the biggest long-term challenge. Young people leaving college toward trades. He is building a training center and retains non-union talent with a generous 401k and year-end bonuses; unions offer a clear pension/medical/raise path.

**Permitting note (matters for the thesis):** On commercial work, permitting, zoning and town approvals are handled by the GC/developer upstream and are NOT his pain ("they handle the towns, the zoning... that's easy"). Only on residential service work does his company "drive the bus" on the township zoning and permit process.

**Other texture**
- Bids off conceptual, roughly 60% drawings not yet town-approved, gives a number by units, then value-engineers to the agreed budget.
- Escalation clauses were negotiated (first 3% on GC, 3-6% split, 6%+ on developer), but one GC demanded port/bill-of-lading proof of tariff hikes (he now uses that tactic himself).
- Bidding a year out he uses the higher projected commodity cost; maintains a large warehouse to buy and store materials when prices are low.
- Prefers GCs and developers he has worked with for 20-25 years; leaves capacity for them.
- Residential service provides daily cash flow that offsets the construction receivables drag; seasonality: HVAC busiest in summer, shoulder months in late summer and late winter. Internal shared-labor program lets departments lend crews.
- Mobilization disputes: GC wants 4% instead of 5% mobilization, only counts duct work onsite, cuts billing (for example $80k to $70k).
- Some GCs now put BIM/CAD modeling and coordination responsibility into sub contracts without paying for it.

**Interviewer framing:** Interviewer 1 said they had done about 70 conversations in 1.5-2 weeks with contractors, developers, subs and owners and visited the Houston permit center, city hall and a county office. Biggest pain heard: getting permits; the overarching theme is delays (everyone burns cash on lean, interest, dumpsters, wages).

**Buy signal:** None. Pure learning; he is an interviewee, not a prospect for permitting software.

**Offered:** developer introductions and a site visit.
