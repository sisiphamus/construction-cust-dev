# Developer A (Houston multi-segment developer) - permitting inconsistency across cities; simple software wins

## Takeaways for analysis

- Validates permitting as a pain but reframes it: the bottleneck is municipal interpretation inconsistency, not code knowledge ("pretty much everyone knows the code"). He wants something like a "common application" for permits across cities.
- Permitting is NOT a 2-year problem in Texas (4-5 months), which tempers the severity of the wedge in this market.
- Strong signal that jobsite workflow software must be dead simple; complexity kills adoption.
- Adjacent/competitor: he is selling his own construction software (payment/AP automation plus jobsite photo verification) and says he is not making much progress selling it.
- Has not looked at any permitting software; no buy signal.

## Conversation notes

**Why industrial now:** Sees a big e-commerce trend and a coming robotics trend (robots not quite here yet, but over ~10 years); robots need warehouses/warehouse-manufacturing facilities. His first warehouse was 26 years ago; now going big via an industrial arm.

**Prefab/modular homes:** Many have tried; it has not worked, including his own attempts. Texas labor is cheap so on-site beats prefab on cost; prefab has a better chance in the Northeast and West Coast, but generally has turned out more expensive. A shrinking young blue-collar workforce could speed robotics. "The biggest problem that we have in this country, no one wants to work." He expects robotics to bring outsourced jobs back.

**Permitting:** "The problem is every municipality is different." Everyone interprets code a little differently and has a different submission process. Example: a large townhouse project in a Dallas-area suburb would not allow an elevator shaft that every other city allowed. Another large project in a Houston suburb faces mind-boggling scrutiny: he probably wasted 4-5 months waiting on a preliminary plat, with hundreds of emails on planning alone, even though officials there admit their process is crazy. He asks why they cannot give comments and approve the preliminary plan and let him finish details at final plan. Something allowed in Houston is refused in a suburb. A universal code would help; "almost like a common application" for college. Nobody has built it. Permitting is generally "not a huge problem for us in Texas... four months delay, five months delay, but not two years."

**Market conditions:** Industrial still holding up; apartments very slow; residential slow; commercial "here and there." He does not use market-data aggregators or consultants ("old school," figures it out himself). On AI: "fantastic... beyond good," and uses a lot of it.

**His software:** Two construction ventures, really internal systems used 20-25 years and tweaked, with AI added in the last 2-3 years. (1) Payments: can take over an accounts payable department. (2) Real-time photo documentation of jobsite progress, e.g. a delivery driver photographs a sheetrock drop and it is counted, so a "dropped 20 sheets, found 18" dispute cannot happen. He does residential and some commercial in-house; on larger industrial projects, investors will not allow in-house builds. Existing users like it. He has spent roughly several million dollars on user experience polish and has patents, but "we're not setting the world on fire" and needs to get better at selling.

**Why build vs buy:** Nothing quite like it on the market; his is "so much better"; trades are not sophisticated so it is dumbed down so "anyone can use it" with no training. Enterprise systems (SAP-style) need training schools, consultants and staff. Basic version is enough for most, with upgrade tiers available. The interviewers said contractors they spoke to found a popular competing product too complicated: "That's the key." Jobsite workers want something simple; "they don't want to spend five minutes to learn anything."

**Ask and next step:** He gave the interviewers his product site and asked for honest feedback as repayment for his time; offered to meet again. When asked for an intro to the developer of his software, he said there is no single developer (the team has been together 20+ years and everyone has a part).

**Interviewers' ideas shared:** aggregating permit outcomes/approvals by municipality so users need not guess policy; a developer wanting aggregated market/pipeline data (currently updated only every six months); a firm wanting marketing channels consolidated into one tool.
