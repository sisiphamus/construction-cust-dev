# Logistics Contact A (former beam-mill scheduler) - structural steel supply chain

Interviewer 1 ran this industry discovery call (construction / structural steel).

## Role and scope

About 30 years at a large US beam mill in the Mid-South, scheduling the mills. She worked exclusively on beams, not sheet, so her knowledge covers beam production and the construction supply chain, not sheet, bolts/fasteners, or fabricator-side end use.

## Beams vs sheet

- **Sheet:** wide consumer and industrial use (siding, appliances, cars); about 500 grades but only about 3 products. Sheet mills care about end use because steel must be exact for the application.
- **Beams:** structural (buildings, bridges, piling, sheet piling); about 5 grades but about 200 products (6-inch to 40-inch beams; a 14-inch beam can weigh nearly 800 lb/ft). Beam mills generally do not care about end use; the fabricator owns that decision.
- Specialty shapes (sheet piling, channels) are slower and harder because they cool at different rates than standard wide-flange beams.

## Supply chain

Scrap, melt shop, roll mill, fabricators/service centers, construction.
- The mill is an enormous energy consumer: electric arc furnaces on electricity, roll mill and reheat furnaces on gas; roughly $1M a month each on electricity and gas at her plant.
- The mill sells to fabricators and service centers, never directly to construction firms, because constructors move in and out of steel (sometimes using concrete) while fabricators are always buying.
- Fabricators turn raw beams into building components (girders, bolted plating, floor systems) and sell to large construction firms. Smaller regional fabricators are served by distributors rather than the mill directly.

## Mill structure and capacity

- Two mills on the site: one for smaller, common beams every job needs (service-center style); one for giant specialty beams.
- It is the parent company's only large beam mill and ships coast to coast. Some beam sizes cannot be produced anywhere else in the US.
- Nominal capacity about 2.4M tons a year; she estimates real output is closer to about 2M because specialty work lowers throughput. They do not expand because demand is not constant; winter slows construction-driven demand.

## Ownership and workforce

- Majority-owned by a US steel parent with a foreign technology partner holding the minority share; the partner sold the beam-making technology, rotates staff through, and is paid on every beam produced.
- Workforce is mostly American, drawn from a wide radius because the host town is small and shrinking. The metallurgy department was the most internationally staffed, driven by heavy testing requirements (e.g., Charpy testing for bridges and large buildings).
- Bonuses: salaried staff earn a return-on-assets bonus (about 3%-30% depending on the year); hourly production staff earn 80%-200% based on good material moved through the mill. A sales manager's effective job is to keep the mill full.

## Competition and pricing

- Main competitor for big beams is a large international producer; cheap imports underpin trade/dumping suits. The foreign competitor requires buying a full ship; her mill allows smaller quantities (still bundles, around 20-ton minimums).
- Domestically, the parent displaced older producers, then faced new entrants.
- Her main critique: drift toward "service center" behavior. The mill has relaxed old standards (cut lengths, non-standard orders) to chase tonnage. Competitors force concessions, for example a landlocked competitor offering barge-equivalent rates on rail shipments, forcing the mill to match. Large customers learn to leverage this, sometimes lying about quotes they received, but are too big to refuse. She favored pushing back, but sales managers will not because their mandate is keeping the mill full.

## Geography and logistics

- Her county hosts three large mills in one place (combined river, rail and truck access) and is one of the poorest in its state.
- Ships by rail, truck and barge. Rail is largely unaffected by snow; trucks heading north require tarping in winter. Construction-driven demand slows in winter (you cannot drive H-piling into frozen ground).

## Relevance

- Material delays: no direct signal on current shortages. Closest data point: large jobs such as big warehouses consume heavy steel and H-piling and can tighten a mill's schedule quickly.
- Data centers pulling steel: limited insight; a beam mill does not track end use. She speculated data-center demand may lean toward foundry or technical parts rather than structural beams, but was unsure.
