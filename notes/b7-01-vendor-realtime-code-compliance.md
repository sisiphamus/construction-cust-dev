# Vendor Founder A (AEC software startup) - real-time code compliance, human stamp stays

## Summary

Vendor Founder A is CEO and co-founder of a 3-person, bootstrapped architecture-software startup. He trained as an architect in the UK, reached the top of the ladder at a large firm within about three years, and left with two colleagues after the firm refused to give them equity in an internal product venture. The product began as the founders' own scripts (Rhino, Grasshopper, Revit, Dynamo) for urban feasibility studies and competitions, packaged into a web platform. Positioning: a "spell-checker for architects." Existing compliance tools (OpenCodes, codecomply.ai, Revit plugins, AI PDF checkers) run after the design is exported; this one flags a violation in the scene as you sketch (for example, drawing seven stories where the limit is five). The company is bootstrapped, nearly took a round a year earlier and declined, and uses AI agents heavily for ops, sales and marketing while founders remain decision-makers.

## Key points

**Why compliance gets skipped early (the pain)**
- In competitions and early design, the brief is square meters plus a national building code plus site-specific regulation. Digging into regulation is slow, and the goal at that stage is to win the client, so teams over-invest in illustrations and facades and only roughly check numbers.
- Competition work is effectively unpaid or underpaid (work for free hoping to win, or a fixed fee everyone overruns), so everyone is already losing money and working over hours; there is no time to check everything.
- Result: firms have won competitions with buildings that, once looked at carefully, were not buildable under the requirements. Intuition-driven massing ("place some volumes, it looks good") is the root.
- Vendor's differentiation claim: real-time checking during design versus a week-later check after export.

**Vendor's go-to-market lessons**
- Founders had no business training and fell into early traps: hired fast, ran out of money, put all eggs in one basket, were bankrupt (in their words) at least three times but recovered because they stay independent.
- They did not understand how to sell to architects and spent too long on big firms with roughly 1-year decision cycles; some segments close in about 3 months. Not knowing the cycle length means you give energy to people who will not pay before you are out of money.
- Investors in incubators pushed "AI" in the pitch for two to three years; now the investor fashion has moved to robotics because AI is a commodity.
- Founder-mode view: even with a large round, founders should stay hands-on in sales, marketing and customer success so they can judge delegated work. He applies the same idea to AI agents: automate "the monkey job" but keep humans deciding. Models only became really good in the last several months, so the processes are fresh.
- Prefers a small, close-knit company over a 100-person pyramid; says advisers told him a product for a complex industry needs a big business, and they want to challenge that with AI leverage.

**Human stamp and AI**
- "There will always be a need in our society for someone to take the blame." A licensed human must review and be responsible even if AI generates 99% of the work; the open question is whether the human layer is the last 1% (the stamp) or the last 30%.
- Interviewer asked whether the company could stamp instead of an individual. Answer: a company is always represented by a human, and no firm will put itself at risk stamping what it does not understand; society will also not accept it because buildings with people inside collapse.
- AI could mass-produce housing blocks (~90% AI-created) where there is a housing crisis, but prominent city-center buildings will stay architect-designed.
- He argues a big tech company with a billion dollars still would not be a competitor for 3-5 years, because without architects there are a thousand places to get buildings wrong. Senior architects are paid more because of tacit knowledge; the product tries to "code" that knowledge into algorithms so others can reuse it.

**Robotics**
- Seen at European trade fairs: robots that paint apartments and drill holes precisely, working overnight on site. He sees construction-tech focused on cost optimization on site, while his company is "design tech."
