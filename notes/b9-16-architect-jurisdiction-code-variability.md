# Architect A (Southern California architecture firm) - jurisdiction-specific code interpretation

## Jurisdiction-specific interpretation

- The state architects' association pushes for consistency, but codes are interpreted differently by jurisdiction.
- One Orange County city is exceptionally strict and requires separate detail sets just for it. New York City also needs different detail sets.
- Houston: inconsistent enforcement vs code; reviewers claim "by the book" but apply subjective interpretation.
- Miami: a significant source of frustration for architects.
- Gray-area problem: codes are often vague, leaving discretion to building officials.

## Current workflow and competitive advantage

- The firm maintains separate detail sets for each difficult jurisdiction. New employees are trained by tradition, not checklist.
- Applying overly conservative details to other cities prices the architect out (contractors tell owners about unnecessary costs).
- Relationship strategy: meet building officials to challenge interpretations, though officials have the final say.
- Client lock-in: a client in the strict city depends heavily on the firm to navigate; the firm knows which reviewers are good or bad.
- The firm deliberately withholds full knowledge from newer or out-of-region architects to maintain its competitive moat. Developers who hire architects across regions without local knowledge become dependent on the original firm.
- Some developer clients are very detail-oriented and needy and would consume all resources; the firm turns down projects to preserve service for good clients.

## Interviewer's data pitch (permitting intelligence)

- Example problem: a Houston developer had a reviewer demand a particular building color despite not being the owner, with no transparency into reviewer psychology.
- Approach: aggregate data from developers, municipalities and reviewers to map average time to acceptance per reviewer, the codes each office consistently red-tags, and permitting timeline predictions. Value: real timelines (8 months vs 3 months) backed by data; claims like "two times faster approval" with evidence rather than vague claims.
- Interviewer 1: "We believe it's a matter of data. If you can aggregate enough data for each reviewer, you'll have greater transparency into the process."
- Tension: empowering other architects with jurisdictional data helps the industry but erodes the firm's moat. Knowledge is commoditizing; a data approach could shift the moat from people to platform.

## Decisions and actions

- Pursue the permitting-intelligence data aggregation platform.
- Collect reviewer-pattern data (strict Southern California city, Houston cases); build a database of timelines and red-tag trends; follow up by email with the firm's business lead (offered); test monetization or advisory services.
