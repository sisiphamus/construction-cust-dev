# MEP Engineer A (Houston MEP firm) - owner standards, budget overruns, relationship-driven work

## Owner standards and the design flow

- Most clients have multiple builds and know what they like. They keep new work within the framework of what they already have, because their maintenance staff must find it familiar. A university posts its design standards online; some school districts have much longer standards because they do not know what to say, so consultants write them and they over-build.
- CSI is the national organizational structure for documents (divisions of specs that go with the drawings), so everyone uses the same scheme. Owners say which guidelines they want and where; they also have opinions on HVAC zoning, and the engineer finds the cheapest zoning approach. Owners dictate equipment types (for a long time one owner wanted only double-duct systems, which no one else in town used).
- Once specs exist, the architect is the head of the contract and disseminates down. Schematic design goes out with basic renderings; MEP adds brief descriptions for anything the owner may have opinions on (manufacturers). Someone does a rough budget to check it is within guidelines: "we have been there a lot where the wallet isn't big enough."
- Design development is where equipment gets sized and major rooms and routing are decided; owners and maintenance staff review at that point. K-12 districts have their own department handling data needs district-wide, wanting data rooms open to the hallway even when grouping would be more convenient.
- Architects differ in how well they control the process: some tell owners "you can't have that in the budget," some do not. On one current project the first version came out over budget, the owner demanded "find the money," and large redesigns followed.

## Estimating

- A good estimator does not let the price increase later: they pad slightly and assumptions tighten. Many estimators do not consider MEP for a school and think of an office building, which has lower MEP cost.
- Costs increase when the owner can actually see the design and develops opinions. Example: an owner with 10 science labs decided they wanted more art rooms; owners do not understand how much lab-type spaces increase a project, and the person running the project is not the person running the school. The job is figuring out the unsaid things the owner wants.

## Tools

- Everything is documented in Revit now; AutoCAD is used for smaller projects and renovations (no one does small projects in 3D, all new projects are 3D).
- Load calculation software: one older command-line tool nobody uses because it is too complicated, and a major HVAC vendor's load tool; some companies built GUIs over the government energy software but it is clunky. Revit could theoretically compute loads from wall properties, but architects do not model the building in a way that works for MEP, and he is lucky to get any information on wall, roof or window construction. Energy codes set minimum U, R and shading values with a safety factor; owners rarely ask for better, and the architecture firm is paid for drawings.
- A couple of photometric packages are used sparingly; the firm relies on lighting reps and catches their mistakes. Spreadsheets are used for basic calcs, and plumbing is done by hand.

## Getting work

- The architecture firm hires the MEP firm. A district knows certain firms and architects work with them to improve win odds. No marketing; relationships. It gets hard when contacts retire (early-retirement bonuses took out a whole group of friendly contacts). Same in K-12: people leave and the reputation is gone.
- Project-management software layers pile on each other; architecture firms must work with it more than MEP. Revit is not backwards compatible, so everyone has to update to the same version. GC and school use different software and require responses in theirs.
