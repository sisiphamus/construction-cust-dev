# Expediter A (Austin residential permit expediter) - code knowledge plus reviewer relationships

## Who
Owner of a small Austin permit expediting and design business, 14 years in the work, no formal technical education (learned on the job). Interviewer 2 reached out as a Rice applied-math student doing customer development on permitting.

## The business
- Started as a "glorified courier": when paperwork had to be printed and walked to the city, GCs and architects had no time to do it.
- Watching rejections and architects' fixes taught him the code; he became a consultant ("the city rejected for this reason, here is how to fix").
- Now a permit plus design plus engineering shop, brokering a third-party drafter and structural engineer. Origin: a pool builder brought bad plans, Expediter A offered to fix them for first-time approval and negotiated a cut; it became an advertised revenue line.
- ~95% residential; turns away commercial ("its own beast", deep electrical/mechanical/plumbing). Currently consulting for a large builder on a 270-home project ("we are their permit monkeys").
- Client mix by volume: GCs first, architecture firms second, homeowners third.

## Why projects get kicked back
- Number one is code non-compliance, not missing documents. Austin-specific traps: 40% lot coverage cap (site plan and coverage calcs), protected trees over 19 inches, pool rules (enclosure, electrical bonding, door/window alarms), accessory structures over 200 sq ft trigger full code (engineered foundation, framing, wind bracing), and garage placement (cannot face more forward than the front of the building; if attached, offset back).
- His own kickbacks usually happen when a builder goes cheap and hires an out-of-town submitter who does not know local rules, or when a homeowner buys a house with unpermitted prior work that must be fixed retroactively.

## Code versus people
- Value is both code knowledge and reviewer relationships. Reviewers prefer submitters who know the code; with his name on an application, reviewers are more inclined to move it because he back-checks his work and delivers clean, well-formatted, code-correct documents.
- Relationships were once in person ("buy the whole box of candy bars"); now mostly digital via portal and email. Reviewers turn over, but repeat dealings build digital reputation.
- The code is public, but knowing what to look for is the hard part; he built the muscle over years of rejections, not by reading the code cover to cover.
- Loopholes used: fire-rating the garage (20-minute self-closing door) to isolate its area and keep a house under the 3,600 sq ft fire-sprinkler threshold when no hydrant is within range; and Texas Chapter 245 vested rights to grandfather an old-platted lot against later restrictive zoning (used on a steep lakefront lot that otherwise had zero buildable area). Works best in high-red-tape jurisdictions.

## Geography
- Austin is the breadwinner and busy. A DFW expansion failed (cold calling, no digital marketing, and little red tape there so expediting is worth less). Has not tried Houston (no zoning, "weird" restrictions, no bandwidth).
- Key signal: less red tape means less value for an expediter, so acute and monetizable pain concentrates where code is most convoluted.

## Read
Strong supply-side confirmation of the permitting thesis: the moat is hard-won jurisdiction-specific knowledge plus trusted reviewer relationships, both things software could encode. He is the manual incumbent and an intro node, not a sales prospect.
