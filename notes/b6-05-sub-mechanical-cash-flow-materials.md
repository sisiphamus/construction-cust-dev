# Sub Owner A (NJ mechanical subcontractor) - cash flow, materials, labor; permitting not his pain on commercial

## Who
Owner of a non-union mechanical sub running three trades (HVAC, electric, plumbing) in the NJ/NYC metro. Roughly half commercial new construction and half residential service; residential gives daily cash flow that offsets the commercial receivables drag. Interviewer 1 had done ~70 conversations in about two weeks, visited the Houston permit center and city hall, and was learning the problem before building.

## Ranked pains (his words)
1. Payment and cash flow is the #1 hindrance. Subs are "the bottom of the heap," front $80k+ on mobilization and wait 45-60 days (some over 90). He has ~$30M owed "on the street." Developers are holding cash longer as financing tightens. Costs up 60-100% over six years (labor $30/hr to $40/hr for a four-year hand, trucks $35k to $65k, medical up about $1M, insurance volatile). Trickle-down: his vendor (an HVAC equipment manufacturer) wants $600k on time and pressures or surcharges him.
2. Material and supply volatility plus tariffs. Prices up since COVID; tariffs are "an excuse"; copper, transformers, generators, switchgear up with data-center demand. No transparency into manufacturer pricing: you can only vet a 15% hike by asking other manufacturers. Regional pricing differs even 40 minutes apart. He prefers one manufacturer for service and parts "cradle to grave" (15-20 year life).
3. Skilled labor: finding, training, keeping people. Building a training center, using 401k and year-end bonuses to retain non-union talent; unions offer a clear pension/medical/raise path. Prefers growing people on a 1-2 year path. Internal "work share" program lends labor between departments with an internal line item.

## Payment mechanics
- AIA pay application: $1M job example, ~5% mobilization; schedule of values negotiated; GC redlines the draw ("mobilization should be 4%"), cuts $80k to $70k; formalize by the 25th, GC signs, owner/developer gets bank green light; 45-60 days to cash.
- Bonds: estimation, performance, payment. Performance bond on a ~$65M three-trade job ran ~$750k; weaker companies might pay 2% (hundreds of thousands more). He tried to bill the bond as a change order; owner offered cost without markup (change orders usually 10 and 5 or 10 and 10).

## Price and contract tactics
- Bid ~10 months earlier; underbidders did not foresee rising costs; renegotiation had some success with an understanding owner (requests to hold price when his costs were up $500-700k failed).
- Escalation clauses: first ~3% on GC, 3-6% split, 6%+ on developer; one GC demanded port bill-of-lading proof of tariff increases.
- Bids off conceptual ~60% drawings not yet town-approved; places a placeholder number by units, then value-engineers to a budget.
- Bids a year out at higher projected copper price and captures savings if prices fall; maintains a large warehouse to buy and store when prices are low.
- Asked if clients demand price cuts when materials fall: "Show me when prices ever came down."
- GC-side shift: contracts increasingly require models/CAD coordination budgets, a major slowdown source; coordination ownership is unresolved.

## Permitting
Commercial: "they handle the towns, the zoning... that's easy"; by the time he bids, zoning/permits/payment are resolved. He only drives the permit process on residential service work (township zoning and permits). Interviewer 1 relayed that permits was the top pain heard elsewhere and delay (interest, lean costs, dumpsters, wages) the overarching one.

## Relationships and workflow
Backlog ~2 years; favors GCs and developers who have treated him well over unknown bidders; 20-25 year relationships; some clients tell him to stop bidding for competitors. Seasonality: busiest in summer; shoulder months late Aug-Sept and Feb-mid-March.

## Buy signal and next steps
None; he is an interviewee, not a prospect for permitting software. He offered developer introductions, a site visit, and his phone number. Follow up for the developer intros.
