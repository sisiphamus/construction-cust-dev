# City Planner A (large Texas city planning department) - why applicants fail on plats and variances

Walk-in access: the development-services floor takes visitors weekdays during business hours with no appointment ("planner of the day"), unlike the innovation director's office, which requires an appointment. Interviewers brought donuts and introduced themselves as students. Recording had no speaker labels.

## The surveyor in the waiting room
- A land surveyor from a national engineering firm, waiting for a recordation appointment, said his current plat took about two years because the prior application lapsed.
- After getting an application number you have one year to record the plat. Lapse causes: owner not paying property taxes, unpaid fees, unresponsive parties. The city requires a tax certificate showing $0 owed before recordation; owners of 20-acre parcels with $20-50k tax bills often cannot or will not pay on time. Lapses peak November to February.
- His firm is hired to clean up developer/owner messes (repay fees, resubmit, push to recordation). Land ownership is fluid; some platt before closing to de-risk timeline.

## Why applicants fail (the core)
- The city has no zoning, so people assume they can do what they saw elsewhere ("that lot got split into three, I'll do the same"). This is the number one trap.
- Deed restrictions (can run with the land indefinitely or auto-renew every 10 years) override what you want: setbacks, single-story only, commercial only, even paint color. Planning does not set them; they surface through the required title report.
- Other blowups: floodplain drainage plan (stamped by floodplain management, about a month), easements/pipelines you cannot build on, larger setbacks off major thoroughfares.
- Sequence of pain: buy land, pay for survey and architect design, submit; the city then finds the restriction/setback/drainage issue; the design does not fit, a roughly one-month clock is running, and a restart burns fees. "Half our job is to find the mistakes."
- Outcomes: downscope (two homes to one), switch use to something unwanted, or abandon and resell. First-timers often blame the city; staff say "I can point to the rule."

## Variances
- If you cannot meet a rule, apply for a variance. Small asks (setback 25 ft to 20 ft) with good justification (walkability, pipeline easement eating half the lot, public good) can pass; big asks (25 to 5) almost never. Purely financial justification ("more lots to sell") is not accepted.
- Decided by an ~18-member planning commission (developers, architects). Staff only recommend; the commission can go either way. About 50-50, and public hearings add weeks. Staff compare it to arguing in court.

## Who fails vs who does not
- Big firms: different issues (street spacing, parking, entrances on 200-acre jobs), rarely rookie errors; dedicated staff, 15-30 years of repetitions.
- Mom-and-pops and first-timers: two-person shops that do not know what they do not know. They get crushed.

## Economics
- Delay equals money: liens and loan interest; an extra week or two can be about $10k; a 2-month job slipping to 6 months adds real interest. Developers come in "yelling," some go over staff to the director.

## Department map
- Development services (plats, land use, variances) is about a dozen people and the busiest group; other groups cover historic, community sustainability, GIS and city real estate. Permitting is a separate center; together they handle about 90% of development.

## Assessment
- Reinforces that the acute, repeatable pain sits with infrequent, first-time, small applicants blindsided by process knowledge. Experts (consultants, expediters) already monetize it. Open question: who pays (owner, developer, consultant) and whether willingness to pay is large enough. The planner is a reusable inside contact. Office staff, not buyers.
