# Security Vendor Reps (access-control manufacturer + installer) - fire marshal inconsistency, AI as a tool

## Summary

A group conversation at a Houston multifamily conference with a salesperson (Vendor Sales Rep A), a technical rep with a machine-learning and former travel-tech background (Vendor Rep B), a couple of other colleagues, and an installer (Installer A) from a regional security-monitoring company that installs the manufacturer's products. The manufacturer does security, access control, video surveillance and visitor management, and has expanded into software (including a residential-tour product it acquired). Two interviewers took part (Interviewer 1 and Interviewer 2).

## Decisions

None.

## Actions

- Exchange professional-network connections with the vendor and installer contacts. Soft, no commitment.

## Key takeaways

- **The fire marshal is the real gate on fire/life safety, and it is discretionary.** "The fire marshal basically has the last say." The standard exists but is loosely worded, so marshals fill gaps with personal preference, and the same marshal may rule differently in different suburbs ("because it feels like it"; one rule in one Houston-area suburb, a different one in a neighboring suburb). Roughly 30-40% of projects need a redesign or fix to satisfy the marshal, and if the marshal does not sign off, nothing moves. It is not extremely frequent per site, but it happens. Turnaround right now is not quick.
- **Counties/municipalities are slow with no incentive to move faster** ("they get to it whenever they wanna get to it"). Reinforces the civil-servant theme in the other conference notes.
- **Online-permitting maturity varies a lot by city.** A college-town city has a fully online system; one rep (a former roofer) could pull a permit remotely there, while Houston requires going in person. Houston has tried to go fully online but moves slowly. The interviewers noted that government adopts technology far slower than even the old private-sector processes panelists complained about.
- **AI-as-tool framing (strong, repeated):** several people argued "AI" is not a product but a tool; a real business solves a specific problem (for example, makes a piece of paper or documentation more efficient) and merely uses AI to do it. They warned about adoption burden ("I now need to get my entire workforce to adopt your AI"; it has to integrate easily). One said the right mindset is "don't build for the current version, build for what's here a year from now." Another distinguished using AI (a chatbot made a grocery list) from utilizing it (behavioral analysis, face match, deep learning). The vendor has used machine learning for ~10 years. A salesperson uses a chatbot to research what projects local developers are working on, which helps the sales game.
- **Interviewers' pitch in the room:** started as help with permitting, evolved into a closed-loop "AI operating system" that tracks every interaction (emails, meetings, social posts) and learns from outcomes; the value is in what it learns, not the raw output. They discussed cold outreach as a moving target: people now distrust over-polished AI-written emails and prefer authentic-looking ones with minor imperfections.
- **Context restated by interviewers:** they are Rice students (just finished first year) who came to the permitting problem via a retail developer who said "permitting" ~100 times in an hour as his greatest pain.
- Interviewers rated the conversation as useful; one takeaway noted at the end was about who to reach out to on the engineering/architecture side (cut off in the recording).
