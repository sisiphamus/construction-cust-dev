# City Permitting Official A (large Texas city permitting center) - full process walkthrough and the missing feasibility gate

Note: the original transcript was very long; this is a structured digest.

## Who

Senior official at a big-city permitting center. Came up through the field, was a plan reviewer, ran the plan reviewers' office, technically retired from the city years ago but remains in a senior role. Runs the city's pre-development meetings; bilingual and personally conducts meetings in Spanish for foreign developers. The interviewers visited in person. High-value insider for the permitting bet.

## The full high-rise path

**1. Replat + Planning Commission (~5-6 months).** Developers often buy land made of several recorded tracts and must combine them (a replat) through the city planning department. A replat triggers public notice and a Planning Commission hearing. This is essentially the ONLY point where the public can object to a project, and even then the commission only considers removing recorded lot lines, not what is built. High rises and gas stations draw pushback. Plan on ~6 months working with a city planner and civil engineers.

**2. Concurrent reviews (start before/with drawing submittal).** Water: apply for a letter confirming infrastructure capacity AND available capacity for the new units. Traffic: submit an access application; trip generation usually triggers a traffic study. These run in parallel with the main submittal.

**3. Plan review (~15 business days per cycle, ~4 cycles, ~6 months).** Standard is 15 business days regardless of size (a doctor's office and a 30-story high rise get the same clock). Expect ~4 cycles, ~60 business days, roughly 6 months. Intake routes the project to every needed department (electrical, mechanical, plumbing, fire marshal, water, structural, signs, etc.), up to 13-15 per project. A dashboard count of ~2,000 projects really means ~10x that many individual reviews. The city uses SPECIALIZED reviewers per trade (many from the field) rather than the single "4-hat" reviewer/inspector model smaller jurisdictions use. Texas plumbers are state-licensed, so plumbing is its own discipline.

**4. Fees.** 25% deposit up front, remaining 75% on approval. It is a deposit, not an extra fee, and the 25% is charged once even if rejected multiple times.

**5. Contractor registration.** GC, electrician, plumber and mechanical contractors must all register with the city.

**6. Inspection phase + phasing.** Projects can be split into multiple permits: horizontal/sitework (underground utilities, paving), foundation/piers (10-50 ft deep), then superstructure; subterranean parking can be foundation up to grade then superstructure. Phasing keeps the field on schedule, but the risk is GCs calling inspections on the wrong project number when many are open. His advice: open the FEWEST project numbers you can. Inspections are NEXT-DAY (schedule before 2:30pm, inspector arrives tomorrow).

**7. CO / TCO.** A certificate of occupancy is required to occupy (analogy: a driver's license for the building). Developers often want to occupy before completion, which means a Temporary CO; exiting elements (stairs, shafts, fire protection) must be in place. His phrase: get "the rabbit out of the building" before releasing floors.

## Why the city is fast (the edge)

- **Electronic plan review** (a commercial plan-review platform, ProjectDox). Moved from paper to online around pre-COVID, so they never missed a beat in COVID. He ran a controlled launch and asked architect friends to try to break it; the first online permit was a cell tower filed by an out-of-state user who found the unadvertised system.
- **Pre-development meetings.** Offered here; New York and Chicago do NOT offer them. He runs them "selfishly": educating developers before submittal makes both sides faster. He triages by questions and pulls only the relevant departments, or routes simple questions to the right person.
- **Speed vs peers.** New York permits per borough, with high rises routed to a central hub and fire districts adding complexity; NY inspections take 1-2 weeks. A California team faced 6-8 months for a traffic variance that this city turns in 4-6 weeks. The city has historically led the country in single-family permits.

## City specifics / "backdoor zoning"

- **No zoning.** But de facto "backdoor zoning" via specific permits, e.g. a liquor store cannot sit within ~1,000 ft of a church or daycare. A downtown ballpark vs nearby church fight over alcohol sales needed government involvement.
- **Noise/hours.** NO hours-of-operation ordinance, so work can legally run at 2am, but decibel limits apply. Foundation pours happen in the early hours because a monolithic pour cannot dry; ~300 concrete trucks stage on the road, generating noise/light complaints to 311.
- **Flood.** Flood review follows FEMA rules, rigid with little discretion (e.g., required elevation). A 100/500-year floodplain is a frequent deal-breaker; a floodway is essentially un-buildable; bringing in fill requires mitigation.

## What causes long delays

- Public/political opposition (rare): one high-rise near a university was locked ~2 years; a new developer later bought the permit and restarted. In another case neighbors flagged a long-gone cemetery on the tract; it was confirmed, the developer replatted around the graves and returned to the commission, and neighbors still fought it.
- Funding drying up: Developer A runs out of money, Developer B buys the permit and continues.
- Permit validity: 180 days, extendable once by 180; every inspection auto-extends 180 days from the inspection date, which is how long projects keep going.
- A failed inspection does NOT bounce you back to plan review; only a revision for the specific element (wall moved, product substituted). Stamped plans are legal documents archived ~5 years.
- Big projects are just long: medical-center hospital towers run 2+ years because of equipment assessment. Large events-related work is active now.

## Self-certification (product-relevant)

Architects self-certifying code compliance: not allowed for foundations (they cannot see the rebar). San Antonio has a limited self-cert program for some residential projects and elements. He was skeptical of the city sending a delegation to study it ("milkshake drive-through window" analogy: no separate fast lane for one small review). New York requires a pre-construction meeting with the building department before inspections can be scheduled; this city lacks resources for that.

## Market read (mid-2026)

Construction is slow: interest rates, political climate and a war are making developers hold funds. Residential is way down: ~275 houses in the system vs a peak above 1,000; overall project counts have ranged to ~2,000-4,000. Solar is the growth area with a dedicated review team.

## Pre-development vs due-diligence meetings

- Pre-development: developer is committed and scouting locations (e.g., a coffee chain taking over former fast-casual sites with grease traps already in place).
- Due-diligence: many what-ifs, project may not happen. He still meets them and surfaces deal-breakers early: floodplain, water capacity (you may think you can tie into your own right-of-way but must bore under another street, blowing the budget), and sign restrictions (a fast-food chain beside a competitor focused on sign count and height; an overpass can block a sign at allowed height).

## Staffing, pay, why people leave

- People leave managers, not organizations. At the city you inherit a team and cannot pick people. Old-timers stay to retirement; younger staff use the city as a training stepping stone. Consultant firms regularly try to recruit him.
- Pay gap is large: a city plan reviewer starting around $70k could do the same job for a third-party reviewer/consultant starting around $105k+.
- No real training program; knowledge moves by osmosis. New reviewers sit beside old-timers with code books open (he keeps the 2021 code and old legacy codes for cross-reference). New plan examiners shadow an inspector first; new inspectors rotate through plan review. Schools do not teach building code. Where review starts depends on discipline: structural at the site plan, electrical at the one-line diagram/panel schedule, mechanical at air changes/thermostats.
- Pressure is production volume, not life safety, and flows down politically: complaints reach the mayor's office and come back through him ("why has this been six months" when he saw it yesterday). Reviewers leave for lower-stress design-side jobs. Active crunch now on large events and stadium projects.

## Longest / hardest reviews

Structural is the most time-consuming; on the civil side, traffic takes longest. The office splits into "protein" teams (electrical, mechanical, plumbing) and civil teams (detention, traffic studies, right-of-way).

## Traffic as a deal-breaker

Traffic approval can force a left or right turn lane not in the budget, which can kill a project. A small tenant adding a driveway is fine; a restaurant generating more trips can trigger one. Operators will not fund city infrastructure they cannot recoup if they close in a few years.

## Airport tiers (hidden deal-breaker)

The city has three airports, each with three concentric noise tiers (Tier 1 closest). Tiers require sound attenuation (windows, doors, wall assemblies). Real example: a developer spent ~$30-50k on drawings and ~6-8 months in planning for an apartment complex, only to learn at structural review that the site was in Tier 1 and apartments could not be built. Nobody told him earlier.

## No early feasibility gate (KEY for the product)

Interviewers pushed directly: platting, airport-tier checks, flood, water capacity and structural review happen in parallel or late, so a developer can burn months and tens of thousands of dollars before learning a site cannot be built. The official agreed: "everybody dropped the ball, never told this guy." There is no upfront feasibility gate. This is a concrete, repeated pain and a direct opening for a fast upfront eligibility/feasibility check.

## Multifamily performance standard review

Apartments trigger a performance standard review pulling in the fire marshal for fire access. The city uses fire access roads (largest apparatus can get in and set up) rather than painted fire lanes.

## Why the city is faster than small fast-growing towns

Interviewers noted developers along the Houston-to-Dallas corridor hit permitting/inspection delays of months to years in small towns; one large developer was losing ~$100k/day during a two-week stretch. His read:

- Volume-driven efficiency: more reps, years of refinement.
- Separate specialized teams for review vs inspection; small towns often use one "4-hat" person for both.
- Next-day and after-hours/overtime inspections (e.g., a 2am foundation pour) that smaller cities do not offer.
- He thinks delays in other cities lean more on the inspection side than review.
- Small-city quirk: a tree-protection ordinance in one small city can freeze a project.
- Inspectors are assigned to fixed zones, so the same trade inspector knows active projects nearby. City cars carry GPS trackers.

## Office of City Engineer

Connecting to the right-of-way (water/sewer, plan and profile) is a separate review on its own timeline, concurrent with building-code review. Another fragmented entity a developer must satisfy.

## County vs city

The county is "a whole different animal": historically fire-code review plus civil only, no building code; it has added oversight recently but is still lighter. He joked it is easier to build a daycare in the county but he would not put his own kids in one; the city's many reviews mean many eyes, which he equates to safety. He offered an intro to a county chief engineer (former acting director at the city); the interviewers accepted.

## Design phase + architects want code pre-review (product-relevant)

High-rise design takes 1-2 years before the city sees drawings, so the 15-day review is a small slice. Key signal: because architecture programs do NOT teach building code, designers actively want a third party to double-check drawings against code before stamping (they cannot vouch for their own seal). Texas law also requires schools to be reviewed by a municipality enforcing building code; this city performs those reviews (not inspection) because law requires it. Demand signal for an AI code-check aimed at architects and designers, not just developers.

## Misc

- "The building outlasts the review": review time is crumbs versus a building standing 50-100 years. A themed aquarium restaurant took ~8-10 months to permit (jungle finishes triggered flame-spread/smoke-index checks, live animals required zoo qualification, a rooftop helipad drew FAA objections).
- The interviewers framed their interest as why cities and companies scale, tied to the university entrepreneurship center, and mentioned they had just spoken with the president of a mid-size Houston developer and wanted to help that team handle small-city permitting timelines.
