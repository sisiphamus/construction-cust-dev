# Developer C (Houston-area residential land developer) - lowest-bid selection, change orders, and time as the biggest cost

## Deal cycle
1. **Land first** ("the biggest part"): location, and above all being in a good school district. Coastal-island locations are not great. Then physical characteristics: zoning where it applies, flooding and hurricanes, topography.
2. **Numbers:** physical and political checks, then a financial model. Investors want a set IRR or equity multiple; if achievable they put a letter of intent on the land and run feasibility.
3. **Feasibility:** talks with engineers, municipal utility district attorneys and land planners to firm up costs into a working budget. Mid-feasibility they engage homebuilders and set up contracts.
4. **Site work:** detention ponds, raising the site (hauling dirt off costs money), utilities (pipe, electrical), then curb and cover, dry utilities, fine grading, notice of substantial completion, then lots go to builders.

## Choosing homebuilders
- It depends on synergy in the community. A few builders to avoid at all costs; look at the top 10 and ask how good the product is and whether they care if the house is poor.
- A very large builder can cut prices so others lose margins and only it sells, which hurts the neighborhood; the developer cannot limit what a company does, and other builders ask the developer to make the discounter raise prices.
- Choose by product and performance in other communities; "this is a person business". Around 3 builders per community, not 4; at 7 or 8 it becomes "a knife fight" and builders blame the developer.
- Why homebuilders do not self-develop: they are publicly traded and self-developing means many years in the red; the developer specializes in buying land builders do not want on their books.
- Sometimes has to talk to builders' construction crews: they pour so many foundations per week that they forget design guidelines.

## Portfolio and tools
Six projects at different stages (one with residents already in section 2, another not yet paved, another with no dirt moved), each with its own investor base, timeline and return. Everything runs on spreadsheets (different ones for different tracking purposes); he personally handles closings and sends paper to the title company.

## Costs and bidding
- Costs: send out to bid; GCs bid; utility contractors bid. Contracts with GCs are typically fixed after signing, with occasional change orders.
- Selection: "we go for the lowest bidder, never seen it not be the lowest." Long relationships with subs who bid lowest to stay on their projects, then "come back later and add change orders"; some companies do this.
- Fuel costs are being passed on in bids. Labor shortages "absolutely".

## Delays (biggest pain)
- Delays raise interest costs, delay returning money to investors, and give a homebuilder a chance to drop its contract. He is always on site to prevent unnecessary delays.
- Example: a paving sub finished, moved on to other jobs, and when county inspection required redoing the pavement it took weeks to get him back because he had picked up other work. Not frequent, but delays are the main issue.
- "Time is the only asset money can't buy": IRR investors, 5 years in and out, minimum 24% IRR.
