# Georgia group call (commercial GC, residential builder, commercial developer) - permitting pain and politics

## Summary
A ~40-minute Zoom group interview run by Interviewer 1 and Interviewer 2 with a handful of metro-Atlanta contractors and developers, arranged through a relative of Interviewer 1 who connected him to an organizer, who pulled the group together. This is independent customer-dev in the Atlanta market, not Houston. The group confirmed permitting as the dominant headache. Core takeaway: the slow part software might fix (plan/guideline review) is real but is the smaller half; the larger half is political (public opposition, rezoning, election-year commissioners) and relational (you win by hiring the right fixer and paying up). This strengthens the permitting bet and re-raises the same scope caveat an earlier adviser flagged: software solves guideline-vetting, not people and politics.

## Speakers
- Interviewer 1 ran the interview; Interviewer 2 co-interviewed (mentioned having spoken with the director of a large Texas city's permitting offices).
- Organizer A: assembled the group.
- GC A: commercial GC working in several north-metro Atlanta suburbs and counties.
- Builder A: residential / subdivision builder (single-family and large subdivisions) in outer-suburb counties.
- Developer A: commercial developer (grocery outparcel deals, rezonings, suburban sites); the most detailed and most software-skeptical voice, doing projects of $30M+.

## Key findings by theme

**Permitting has structurally slowed, especially post-COVID.**
- GC A: ~14 months to get a land + building permit on a ~6,000 sq ft ground-up commercial job in a small city with no online plan submission that outsources all review to a third-party engineering firm.
- GC A: the old playbook (a paid expediter who could walk a 3,000 sq ft tenant job to a permit in a day or two) is dead. Knowledgeable permit-office staff left during COVID; replacements lack experience and initiative. Even a "simple" small build-out is now 3-4 weeks minimum.

**Small municipalities are now as hard as, or harder than, big cities.**
- Organizer A: small cities "want to look and act like a big city but maintain small-town charm, so they over-regulate everybody."
- Builder A: smaller jurisdictions are getting stricter, partly reacting to neighbors upset about development; sometimes the lever is erosion-control enforcement used to nitpick or shut down a site. "The further south you go, the harder it gets."

**The real bottleneck is politics, not regulation (Developer A's throughline).**
- Public-notice / rezoning hearings are where projects die: anyone within ~1,000 ft of a rezoned parcel gets a letter and can show up to oppose. "No matter what you build, somebody says I don't want it."
- Election cycles are the wild card: a commissioner running for reelection will vote against a project for votes, even when it matches the city's own comprehensive land-use plan. His ~32-acre commercial project fits the comp plan, yet the mayor warned of heavy opposition.
- Countermeasures are relational and expensive: hire a land-use attorney and lobbyist (~$25K cited), rent a restaurant and run an informational with color boards and a traffic study, and offer the adjacent neighborhood concessions (new patio furniture, redone landscaping or signage).

**Stormwater / erosion (NPDES) is a cost and shutdown lever.**
- On a 10-acre tract, site work cost roughly 2.5x the land cost, driven by federal/state stormwater detention and water-quality requirements. "Land development costs blew past land costs two or three years ago."
- Rules make it easy to get into compliance but just as easy for an inspector to find a reason to shut a site down and take control. Government exempts itself.

**The incumbent 'solution' is a fixer, and Developer A doubts software can replace it.**
- "You hire [a known local fixer]... he walks in the back door, gets it stamped, and you pay him whatever you have to pay." In one large county he goes "with a blank check."
- His blunt thesis: "The government does nothing well... there's really not a solution," and reviewers are people "who couldn't make it in the real world." He is the archetype of the AI-skeptical buyer who has made peace with pay-to-play. The incumbent to displace is human expediters/fixers and pay-to-play.

## Relevance
- Strong corroboration of permitting as the #1 pain, independent of Houston.
- Supports a codes/precedent-database thesis (stormwater/NPDES, rezoning, third-party review are knowable rules).
- Scope caveat: software addresses guideline vetting, not politics.
