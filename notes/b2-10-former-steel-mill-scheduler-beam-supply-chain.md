# Former Beam-Mill Scheduler A (large US beam mill) - structural steel supply chain

Note: replaces a raw auto-transcript that garbled terms; interviewer was Interviewer 2 (industry discovery call).

## Background

~30 years at a beam mill in a small Arkansas town, then a six-month return stint after retiring. Core role: **scheduling the mills**. Worked only on **beams**, not sheet, so knowledge does not extend to sheet, fasteners or fabricator-side end use.

## Beams vs. sheet

- **Sheet:** wide consumer/industrial use (siding, appliances, cars). ~500 grades but only ~3 products. Sheet mills care about end use because the steel must be exact for the application.
- **Beams:** structural (buildings, bridges, piling). ~5 grades but ~200 products (6-inch to 40-inch beams). Beam mills generally do not care about end use; the fabricator owns that decision.
- Specialty shapes (sheet piling, channels) are slower and harder to run because they cool at different rates than standard wide-flange beams.

## Supply chain

Scrap -> melt shop -> roll mill -> fabricators/service centers -> construction.

- The mill is an enormous energy consumer: electric arc furnaces run on electricity; the roll mill and reheat furnaces run on gas. Roughly $1M/month each on electricity and gas at that plant.
- The mill sells to **fabricators and service centers, never directly to construction firms**, because constructors move in and out of steel (they sometimes use concrete) while fabricators are always buying.
- Fabricators turn raw beams into building components (girders, bolted plating, floor systems) for large construction firms. Smaller regional fabricators are served by distributors rather than the mill directly.

## Mill structure and capacity

- Two mills on one site: Mill 1 for smaller, common beams every job needs (service-center style); Mill 2 for giant specialty beams.
- It is the parent company's only large beam mill and ships coast to coast. Some beam sizes cannot be produced anywhere else in the US.
- Nominal capacity ~2.4M tons/year; her estimate of real output is closer to ~2M tons/year because specialty work lowers throughput. No expansion because demand is not constant; winter slows construction demand.

## Ownership and workforce

- Majority US-owned joint venture with a Japanese partner that sold the beam-making technology and rotates staff through.
- Workforce is mostly American, drawn from a wide radius because the town is small and shrinking. The metallurgy department was the most foreign-staffed, driven by heavy testing requirements (e.g., impact testing for bridges and large buildings).
- Bonus structure: salaried staff get a return-on-assets bonus (3%-30% of pay depending on the year); hourly production staff 80%-200% based on good material moved through the mill. A sales manager's effective job is to keep the mill full.

## Competition and pricing

- International: a large European producer is the main competitor for big beams; cheap imports from China and Russia underpin trade/dumping cases. The competitor requires buying a full ship; the mill allows smaller quantities (still bundles, ~20-ton minimums).
- Domestic history: the mill put two older integrated producers out of business, then faced new entrants.
- Her main critique, drift toward "service center" behavior: the mill relaxed old standards (allowing cut lengths, non-standard orders) to chase tonnage. A landlocked competitor offers barge-equivalent rates on rail shipments, forcing the mill to match. Large customers learn to leverage this, sometimes misstating rates they were quoted, but are too big to refuse. She favored pushing back, but sales managers will not since their mandate is keeping the mill full.

## Geography and logistics

- One northeast Arkansas county hosts three big steel mills (river, rail and truck access) and is one of the poorest counties in the state.
- Ships by rail, truck and barge. Rail is largely unaffected by snow; trucks heading north need tarping in winter. Construction demand slows in winter (cannot drive H-piling into frozen ground).

## Relevance to research threads

- Material delays: no direct signal on current shortages (she left ~3 years ago). Closest data point: large jobs like big warehouses consume heavy steel and H-piling and can tighten a mill's schedule quickly.
- Data centers absorbing steel: limited insight; a beam mill does not track end use. She speculated data center demand leans toward foundry/technical parts rather than structural beams, but was unsure.
