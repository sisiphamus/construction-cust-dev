# City Planner A (Houston planning, development services) - variances, deed restrictions, why applicants fail

## Setting

Interviewers 1 and 2 walked into the planning and development services floor of a large Texas city's municipal building (donuts plus "we're Rice students," no appointment). They spoke with three people: a land surveyor from a large engineering firm waiting on a plat recordation appointment, another planner/reviewer, and the main interview with City Planner A, a senior / variance planner who reviews subdivision plats and variances. He was friendly and said to email or call anytime. Walk-in access: planning floor, Mon-Fri 8-5, no appointment, ask for the planner of the day (by contrast, another floor requires an appointment). Recording about 39 minutes, a digest of which follows.

## Surveyor thread

- In the waiting room for a recordation appointment. His current plat took ~2 years because the prior application lapsed.
- After you get an application number you have **one year to record the plat**. Lapse causes: owner not paying property taxes, fees unpaid, parties not answering emails/calls.
- The city requires a current **tax certificate showing $0 owed** before a property can be recorded/sold. Owners (especially on 20-acre parcels with $20K-$50K tax bills) often cannot or will not pay on time, stalling everything. Heavy lapses Nov-Feb.
- His firm gets hired to clean up developer/owner messes: repay fees, resubmit already-reviewed documents, push to recordation. Land ownership is fluid; some people plat before they buy to de-risk timeline.

## Why applicants fail (the core)

- Houston has **no zoning**, so people assume they can do what they have seen elsewhere ("that lot got split into 3, I'll do the same"). The wrong assumption is the #1 trap.
- **Deed restrictions** (set by prior owners/subdivisions, can run with the land forever or auto-renew every 10 years) override what you want to build: minimum setbacks, single story, must be commercial, even paint color. Planning does not set them; they surface them via the required **title report**.
- Other surprises: **floodplain drainage plan** (signed/stamped by floodplain management, ~1 month), easements and pipelines you cannot build on, larger setbacks off major thoroughfares.
- Sequence of pain: the applicant buys land, pays for a survey and architect design, submits; the city then finds the deed restriction, setback or drainage issue. The design no longer fits, a ~1 month clock is running, and restarting burns fees. "Half our job is to find the mistakes."
- Result: many downscope (2 homes to 1), switch use (to a commercial use they did not want), or abandon and resell. First-timers often blame the city; "it's everything but personal, I can point to the rule."

## Variances

- If you cannot or will not meet a rule, apply for a **variance**. Small asks (e.g., 25 ft to 20 ft setback) with good justification (walkability, a pipeline easement eating half the lot, public good) can pass. Big asks (25 to 5) almost never.
- Pure financial justification ("I want more lots to sell") is not accepted.
- Decided by an **~18-member planning commission** (developers, architects). Staff only recommend; the commission can go against it either way. About 50-50. Adds time (public hearing, extra weeks).

## Who fails vs who does not

- Big firms: different problems (street/intersection spacing, parking, entrances on 200-acre jobs) but rarely the rookie mistakes; dedicated staff with 15-30 years of reps.
- Mom-and-pops / first-timers: 2-person shops, too much on their plate, do not know what they do not know. They get crushed.

## Economics

- Delay equals money. Liens accrue and loan interest runs: an extra week or two can cost ~$10K; a 2-month job slipping to 6 months adds real interest cost. Developers come in "yelling"; "we've seen tears"; some try to go over staff to the director.

## Departments map

- Development Services (his group, ~12 people, busiest): plats, land use, variances, "the meat and potatoes." Also historic preservation, community sustainability, GIS, and a real estate division (city land/leases). Permitting itself is a separate center. Together planning and the permitting center handle ~90% of development.

## Relevance

Strongly reinforces the wedge thesis: the acute, repeatable pain is on infrequent, first-time and small applicants blindsided by process knowledge (deed restrictions, title reports, drainage, setbacks, variances). Experts (consultants/expediters at engineering firms) already monetize navigating it. A "guarantee first-pass approval / do-it-for-you" service maps onto this population. Open question: who pays (owner vs developer vs consultant) and is willingness-to-pay large enough. The senior planner is a reusable inside contact for the planning/variance leg.
