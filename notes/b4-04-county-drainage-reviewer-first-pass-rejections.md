# County Drainage Reviewer A (large Texas county) - 3.7 submittals to approval; the office acts as educator

Interviewers: Interviewer 1 and Interviewer 2. About 33 minutes. Reviewer background: ~16 years in flood control, ~10 years in private drainage work, now back at the county. This is the permit-office (supply) side of permitting. Buy signal: none, as a government reviewer, but a valuable repeat knowledge source.

## Thesis validation
- **Average 3.7 submittals before a site-development permit is approved.** The worst ~5% submit 10-15 times over months. Most are approved by the 2nd-3rd round. "If you submit 10 times they'll approve it eventually because they get tired of seeing it."
- **Rejections are for knowable, checkable reasons** that a guideline-vetting tool could pre-catch:
  - Review-sheet info not filled out properly (the single biggest bucket). "They should know but don't, and don't put in the effort."
  - Wrong drainage OUTFALL assumption, hence wrong detention rate. Roadside ditch 1.0 acre-ft/acre, storm sewer 0.75, flood control channel 0.65, state DOT roads have their own. Get it wrong and the whole plan is redesigned. Applicants make up their own rate instead of using the one on the review sheet.
  - Draining to a state DOT road without a DOT permit; applicants assume it is a county street.
  - Missing plat: subdivisions of 14+ lots with private streets need a detention fund maintained by a government agency. Applicants do not read the regulation, get a plat done, then cannot say who maintains the fund, and "the project goes in the trash".
  - Incomplete packages submitted just to hit a client's 30-day deadline ("I met my role"); the office is used as the bad guy.
- **The office does the applicants' QA/QC.** "Our reviews are almost like a review training... review, educate, train as we submit." Big engineering firms hand junior EITs the work with no internal QA/QC and say "go figure it out." Mostly engineers submit; expediters are a small share. Some firms consistently go through easily, regardless of size ("depends on the engineer, roll the dice").

## Process detail
- The county only governs DRAINAGE (floodplain/detention) and FIRE; the city handles water and wastewater and the rest. A drainage report is not needed outside a floodplain unless the site is 28 acres or larger.
- A site-development permit covers everything except the building; the fire code permit covers the building and cannot be approved until the site plan is (can be submitted concurrently).
- Site-dev runs through 4 county groups: commercial, public right-of-way, traffic, stormwater quality (sometimes a 5th, on-site sewage). The regional flood control district also reviews every plan set (usually a quick sign-off, ~90% of cases do not touch a channel). It was removed from the county's review groups when the state imposed a 30-day review limit, so it would not add days.
- State-mandated 30-day review of the first submittal (the office hits ~25-30 days). Applicant turnaround ranges from a week to two months; those needing a plat disappear for a month or two and some abandon.
- Inside city limits the plan goes to the city first, then the county; in the ETJ the county approves both permit and plans. All electronic.
- The county asks for a "letter of no objection" from utility and pipeline easement owners (people propose work under high-voltage lines or over pipelines). Pipeline owners are hard to chase.
- No zoning, but PLATTING acts like zoning: newer plats restrict commercial use on residential lots; the appraisal district's "residential" label is not zoning. Applicants get excited about "no zoning", pay an architect for a full plan, then get told they never checked the plat.
- Smaller towns between Houston and Dallas/San Antonio: a three-story residential complex took two years to approve in one case. Small staffs, relaxed or uncertain requirements, and outsourced engineers, with one overloaded outsourced engineer reviewing for several cities (he had to chase daily when in private practice).

## Cascading requirements example (fast-food restaurant)
Restaurant needs fire sprinklers; if on a well (not a municipal utility district water supply) you need a large on-site fire storage tank; if the site was laid out without room for it, it does not fit. The driveway may trigger a required left-turn lane from a traffic study (~$500k). "People lay it all out, get the building permit, then realize they need a storage tank with nowhere to put it."

## His recommended ideal flow
1. Engineering site assessment (~$1,500): jurisdiction, water/wastewater, outfall, depths, floodplain; 1-2 page write-up.
2. Architect schematic sketch (~a couple thousand dollars): parking, layout, does it fit.
3. $600 pre-development meeting with the county and city planning BEFORE drawing the full (expensive) plan set. The meeting tells you sprinklers, water supply, turn lanes. Underused: mostly those with means attend; surprisingly many come with no engineer or architect, just an owner wanting to talk. "You shouldn't have bought it without laying it out."
