# Architect A (small Dallas multifamily architecture firm) - zoning interpretation is the real bottleneck

## Summary
Customer discovery call (Interviewer 1) with a founding partner at a Dallas architecture firm about how zoning and permitting play out for multifamily projects in Dallas. He walked the pipeline (zoning, platting, building permit) and where it breaks: Dallas has the most PDs in the country, the code is not black and white so reviewer interpretation drives everything, and there is no public notice when the city changes an interpretation. His personal fix is paying a former city zoning official turned private consultant to review drawings early and cite code when the city is wrong.

## Key points
- **Single-family and duplexes are easy; multifamily is hard.** Single-family and duplexes are treated as residential with simpler zoning. Multifamily (three or more units, townhomes, apartments) is grouped as commercial and is where the difficulty lives.
- **Dallas has the most PDs in the country.** A PD is custom zoning written for one district, used to densify, create mixed use, or enforce a look (e.g. uniform blockface). City staff juggle hundreds of PDs, each with its own rules and exceptions, and even they find it confusing. MF2 is the common standard district, but a given PD might allow multifamily, single-family, duplexes and liquor stores all together. Some PDs run 800 pages.
- **Townhome replat pipeline runs ~9 to 12 months on its own.** For townhomes (single-family attached, owner owns the land, unlike a condo), a developer buys one lot and replats it into many small lots (e.g. 20). The replat triggers full city review: surveyor and civil work, sewer and water line upgrades because density jumps (e.g. 3 homes becoming 20), fire department checking driveway width and hose reach, site improvements, then the city records the plat at the county. Only then can you submit for building permit.
- **Replat plus building permit is about 15 months.** The building permit is a separate review (height, square footage, fire-rated walls, stairs). Resubmittals always find something.
- **Comments arrive in waves.** When civil turns in drawings (drainage, paving, demo), each department (paving, drainage, sewer, water/wastewater) returns comments in batches, e.g. 30 at a time, some trivial ("your font size is too small"). Civil fixes 30, resubmits, gets 25 more. Standard is about 3 resubmittal rounds (four submissions).
- **Code is not black and white; interpretation drives everything.** Something allowed for years can suddenly be disallowed when a new reviewer reads it differently. High turnover plus COVID-era understaffing made it worse: staff moved between DFW cities with different rules and fell behind.
- **Live example: fire marshal reinterpretation.** A long-tenured fire marshal retired; the new one reinterpreted aerial-apparatus access. For buildings over three stories the fire truck needs a 26-foot-wide clearance. Previously the street counted; now access must run along the long side of the building (the driveway). On common 50-foot lots, after a 10-foot side setback and a 26-foot drive, only ~14 feet remains where 20 is needed for a garage, so projects become unbuildable unless they drop to two stories. A partner's project has been stuck four months; hundreds of projects are stalled. The city is slow to rule because once it decides it must apply the ruling to everyone.
- **PDs can contradict themselves.** On one pre-COVID PD, zoning required a max 10-foot setback (row-house look) while the landscape side required large-canopy trees with trunk center 15 feet from the building. With 10 feet of planting space the math is impossible. The two city groups wrote rules without coordinating. Months of talking to both; resolution was medium trees in planting grates, costing the developer more.
- **No public notice when interpretations change; permit and zoning use different reviewers.** No website announcement; you find out on submittal. A later reviewer can overrule an earlier answer (e.g. a PD said "no landscape requirements," a reviewer later read that as "revert to standard landscape article," opposite of the plain reading).
- **Hidden, unwritten calculation rules.** Height is a trap: under MF2 a 36-foot limit on a gable roof is measured to the midpoint between top plate and peak, but some PDs measure differently or allow nothing above 36, and the method is not written in the PD. The firm found out the hard way.
- **His fix: a private ex-city zoning consultant.** During the contradictory-PD fight he met a former senior city zoning official who knew the code cold and later went private. On complex PD projects he bakes her fee into the client fee. She reviews schematics, catches contradictions and unwritten interpretation traps before drawings are expensive to change, tells him when the city is wrong and which section to cite, and warns when the city has quietly changed a long-standing interpretation. "Saved our butts many times."
- **Interviewer idea: track approval outcomes by reviewer** to predict interpretations, replacing the consultant or a public announcement. He liked it but flagged two limits: you may not be able to look up who reviewed other projects in the city system, and there is no recourse regardless. He has documented neighborhood projects that did the thing he was denied; the city said "we are not talking about those projects, we're talking about yours."
- **City is courteous but overloaded.** ~90% of staff are helpful; the problem is understaffing, plus the city is not a private business so there is no recourse on slow timelines or changed minds.

## Themes
- The bottleneck is interpretation, not paperwork volume: the same plan passes for one reviewer and fails for another.
- PD density multiplies the problem (hundreds of bespoke codebooks with hidden exceptions).
- Time compounds: replat (9-12 months) plus permit with staged resubmittals lands at ~15 months.
- A single new official can freeze hundreds of projects overnight.
- The market's coping mechanism is private expertise: a former insider hired to de-risk in schematics (a manual workflow a tool could replicate).
- Information asymmetry and no recourse define the relationship with the city.

## Quotes
- "These zoning rules are not always black and white. So people interpret them differently. So sometimes you may be doing something that was allowed for many years and then suddenly someone new comes in and interprets it differently and you're not allowed to do that anymore."
- "I can't satisfy the landscape part of the PD and the zoning part of the PD because my buildings can only be 10 feet apart with 10 feet of grass. But I have to plant these large canopy trees that basically need 15 feet clearance all around. So that math doesn't make sense."
- "On complex projects I tell the client, hey, this is a part of the fee that we have to hire her. It's going to save you so much time and money later on."
- "When they change things or their interpretation of things, there's no like public service announcement... when they change something you don't know until you submit, and you've been doing something for 10 years and it works."
- "There's no recourse, the city is the city. They're not like a private business. If it takes them three weeks to get back to you, it takes them three weeks."
