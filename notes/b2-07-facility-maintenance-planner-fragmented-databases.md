# Facility Maintenance Planner A (large federal industrial facility) - wants one database instead of several

Note: informal conversation, not a formal customer interview; identifying personal and site details removed.

## Workflow

- Wears several hats: planner, scheduler, material coordinator, first-line supervisor. The schedule is agreed by multiple organizations and is firm ~2 months out; it tightens as the execution week approaches, and everything promised for that week must get done.
- Currently has a two-week schedule of 81 items and must confirm materials, people and resources exist for each. Around 55 items sit on the docket at one time, some not due for six months.
- Work items come from (a) system-generated preventive maintenance based on past completion dates (verify equipment is unchanged since the last cycle; some items recur weekly, some every five years, some no longer need doing) and (b) corrective maintenance triggered when someone reports a fault (e.g., a pump making a noise).
- Equipment rentals (e.g., a 66 ft man lift) come from an on-site equipment organization that bills from yard departure to return against an annual budget; he plans these months ahead and schedules "at risk" if equipment is not yet confirmed.
- Hazardous-energy lockout procedures and hazard-analysis paperwork are required, scaled to location and work (barriers near power lines, clearing buildings below overhead work). Some knowledge is "tribal": which tasks must be done together is not in the system.
- Daily morning meeting on that day's work and a mid-afternoon status. Maintenance fixes things; operators run them (e.g., swap to a backup fan, then diagnose).
- Cost cap: a 1940s federal prevailing-wage rule limits work per work order (about $2,000, and the allowed hours per work order have been cut from ~90 to ~40); exceeding it needs approval from an administrator and often turns the job over to construction or a subcontractor.
- Contractor scheduling is coordinated through another organization.

## Example of delay

A simple annual HVAC fan replacement showed up on the list four months before execution; it still needed lockout, hazard analysis, a lift, filters, belts, and parts confirmed in stock. Simple jobs in one place become hard elsewhere (radiological facilities add inspections and many organizations); shutting down fans can impact something else; a walk-down may find a parked vehicle blocking the lift.

## The pain (buy-signal framing)

Multiple databases: one for tracking PMs, an asset database with plant information, and separate programs for PM deferrals. He must enter a PM deferral in one program and record it in another, and individually order belts and filters while a receiving person keeps up with part number changes. He wants one database that does it all: blueprints and equipment entered once and flowing into work-request generation. He acknowledged a single system is a big undertaking and a single failure would be a risk ("all your eggs in one basket"). No buy signal; structural/systems pain.
