# Sub Owner A (metro NY/NJ mechanical contractor) - cash flow, materials, labor pains; permitting not his problem

## Digest

**Who:** Owner of a metro NY/NJ mechanical subcontractor running three trades (HVAC, electric, plumbing). Non-union, northern NJ, roughly two years of backlog (order of $200M), ~$12-15M a month of work, tens of millions outstanding in receivables. Roughly 50/50 to 60/40 commercial new construction vs residential service.

**Format:** about 38-minute single-recorder call with Interviewer 1; auto-transcribed, so details may contain transcription errors.

## Ranked pains (his words, condensed)

1. **Payment / cash flow is the number one hindrance.** Subs are "the bottom of the heap," front $80K+ on mobilization and wait 45-60 days (some over 90) to get paid. Tens of millions are owed to him right now. Financing across the industry is tightening and developers hold money longer ("these guys are building a building, they should have the funds in place"). He prides himself on paying his own vendors on time to earn priority and pricing. Payment runs on standard AIA pay applications: for example a ~$1M subcontract mobilizes ~5%; a monthly request of ~$80K is sent against a pre-negotiated schedule of values; the GC redlines it (mobilization should be 4%, "we only see X duct work on site") and cuts it to ~$70K; formalized by the 25th, signed by the GC, sent to the owner-developer, then to the bank for green light. About 45-60 days before cash arrives. His OEM equipment vendor then demands large payments or adds a premium, so it trickles down. Quote: "The number one hindering situation is how poorly subs and trades are paid."
2. **Material and supply cost volatility, plus tariffs.** Prices up sharply since COVID (everything up 60-100% in six years; skilled tradesperson wages from ~$30 to ~$40/hr; trucks $35K to $65K; health insurance up by about $1M). Tariffs are "an excuse" used by manufacturers; there is no pricing transparency, so you can only vet by checking other manufacturers. Copper, transformers, generators and switchgear are up with data-center demand. A ~$65M three-trade contract bid ~10 months ago is now underwater; renegotiation mostly fails ("show me when prices ever came down"). Owners often rebid when he asked for relief, and one GC made him prove tariff pass-throughs with port bill-of-lading line items (he now uses that practice himself). Manufacturers divide the region into pricing zones, so markets 40 minutes apart are priced differently. Escalation clauses: first ~3% on the GC, 3-6% split, 6%+ on the developer.
3. **Skilled labor.** "The obvious one": find, train, keep. Young people dropping out of college toward trades; he is building a training center. Retains non-union talent with a generous 401(k) and year-end bonuses; internal "work share" program lends labor between departments with an internal line item.

## Bonds

Three bonds on bigger jobs: bid, performance and payment. A performance bond on a ~$25M scope costs roughly $250K (under 1%); on a $65M three-trade job his bond is about $750K. A weaker company could pay ~2%. He prices bond cost as a change order; owners offer to reimburse the expense but without markup (normally 10-and-5 or 10-and-10).

## Permitting (matters for the thesis)

On commercial work, permitting, zoning and town approvals are handled by the GC/developer upstream and are "not his pain" ("they handle the towns, the zoning... that's easy"; they just ask the sub for licenses and permit info). He bids off conceptual / ~60% drawings that are not yet town-approved, gives a number by unit as a placeholder, and then value-engineers to the agreed budget. Only on residential service work (HVAC/electric/plumbing into homes) does his company go through township zoning and "drive the bus" on permits.

## Other texture

- Projects come from long relationships with GCs and developers ("20-25 years of relationships"); he does not bid for anyone off the street.
- New requirement from GCs: contractors must fund CAD/model coordination in the contract; "who is taking control of the coordination" is a slowdown.
- Warehouse of about 75,000 sq ft to buy and store materials when prices are low; bids a year out using higher projected copper cost.
- Seasonality: HVAC and construction busier in summer; residential cash flow offsets receivables drag in shoulder months.
- Manufacturer relationship: prefers one HVAC manufacturer for consistency (training, parts, "cradle to grave": install, maintain, repair, replace in 15-20 years).
- Rumored collusion among major HVAC manufacturers; he thinks it is overblown.

## Close

Interviewer 1 explained he and a friend at Rice built companies freshman year and wanted to pivot together, explored every industry and landed on construction. Biggest pain they have heard: receiving permits, but overarching is delay, since everyone burns cash on delays (lean costs, interest, dumpsters, wages). About 70 conversations in 1.5-2 weeks (contractors, developers, subs, owners), plus visits to the Houston permit center and city hall. The contact invited a visit, offered developer introductions.

## Buy signal

None. Pure learning; he is an interviewee, not a prospect for permitting software.
