# Veteran Developer A and Architect A - accuracy is everything, code interpretation

## Summary

Two back-to-back conversations on the floor of a Houston multifamily conference.

**Part 1: Veteran Developer A** (older, self-described "dinosaur", sold his larger company years ago, now runs a small office; buys land in cash; uses max-guaranteed-price GC contracts; knows a Houston multifamily developer's president socially). Core message: for an AI permitting tool the hard part is not reducing time, it is accuracy and trust. AI tools cite sources that still have to be checked, and a wrong answer carries real liability (he raised new AI-liability court cases and insurance products). He confirmed permitting pain: lost roughly a year in San Antonio between submitting plans and getting them finalized (some self-inflicted). Framed it as a civil-servant/people problem made worse since COVID, plus a sequential-reviewer trap: one reviewer approves, the next flags something the first missed, and it takes 2-3 weeks to get back on a reviewer's calendar. Planning is a bit easier than permitting, but planning staff sometimes say you can do something you actually cannot.

**Part 2: Architect A and a colleague.** Design is not their pain; code and permitting are. Codes change about every 3 years, and reviewers interpret them inconsistently (one asked for 2-hour-rated walls that, on discussion, were not required). Requirements that vary city to city and are often unclear in the code text: ADA/chapter 11 accessibility (enforcement varies; Houston may not truly enforce it; type A vs type B units; Houston amendments carve out exceptions), density (units per acre), parking ratios (per 1-bed and 2-bed) and stall dimensions, and pervious vs impervious (green) area. The architect, not the developer, is usually the one who submits to most cities. Houston is still on the 2021 IBC while Austin moved ahead.

## Key takeaways

- **Accuracy and trust are the real bar, not speed.** "Anyone can reduce time, but can you increase the accuracy?" People rely on the output; if AI errs there is liability.
- **Title report pain (adjacent opportunity):** title companies used to review backup documents for relevance; now they hand over every exception document unreviewed. He spent 5-6 hours reading roughly 20 linked exception documents from a 4-page report to find that about 90% did not touch his property, just to brief his lawyer to remove them from the title commitment. The interviewers said they were building software to analyze codes and title reports.
- **How this developer operates:** buys land in cash or closes right before construction (pursuit costs but no carrying loan during entitlement, unlike developers who borrow and pay interest during delays). Max-guaranteed-price GC contracts put overrun risk on the GC; sometimes a shared-savings split. He speculated that the Houston developer president's intensity about permitting reflects carrying loan interest through the delay and being unable to control the timeline.
- **AI-market skepticism:** he has heard "AI" mentioned more at conferences in three months than in all of the prior year. Many ex-tech founders are "throwing darts." Unless the product is genuinely technical, buyers can build it themselves; he cited a company that spent more on tokens than it earned.
- **Interviewer's weekend build:** a queryable database of Houston codes that answers code questions with citations, legal precedent and enforcement, constantly updating. Architect wishlist: enter "200 units in Houston" and get how many units must follow chapter 11 and how strictly (type A/B), plus density and parking outputs.
- Background: the interviewers are university students in a summer innovation program working with a Houston multifamily developer, heading to a national architecture conference next.
