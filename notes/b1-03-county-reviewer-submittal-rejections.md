# County Site-Development Reviewer A (large Texas county) - 3.7 submittals to approval, office acts as educator

Permit-office (supply) side of permitting. Not a buyer, but a strong repeat knowledge source. Transcript was single-channel auto-transcription, so some terms are approximate.

## Key findings
- Average about 3.7 submittals before a site-development permit is approved (about three rejections). Roughly the worst 5% submit 10-15 times over months; most finish by round two or three. "If you submit 10 times they'll approve it eventually because they get tired of seeing it."
- Rejections are knowable and checkable:
  - County review sheet info not filled out properly (largest bucket).
  - Wrong drainage outfall assumption, which changes the required detention rate: roadside ditch about 1.0 acre-ft per acre, storm sewer 0.75, flood control channel 0.65, state highway has its own. Getting it wrong means redesigning the plan.
  - Draining to a state highway without a state DOT permit (applicants assume a county street).
  - Missing plat: 14+ lots with private streets need a detention fund maintained by a government agency; applicants plat first, then cannot name who maintains the fund, "and the project goes in the trash."
  - Incomplete packages submitted just to hit a client's 30-day deadline ("I met my role"), using the county and city as the bad guy.
- The office does applicants' QA/QC: even big engineering firms give junior engineers-in-training the work with "go figure it out" and no internal check. "Our reviews are almost a review training... we review, educate, train as we submit."
- Easiest vs hardest applicants are mostly engineers (expediters are a small share); depends on the individual engineer, not firm size.

## Process details
- County governs drainage/floodplain and fire only; water and wastewater are city. Site-development permit covers everything except the building; fire code covers the building and cannot be approved before the site plan. No drainage report needed outside a floodplain unless 28 acres or more.
- Site-dev permit routes through four groups (commercial, public right-of-way, traffic, stormwater quality; sometimes a fifth for on-site sewage) plus a separate flood control district sign-off (usually quick, about 90% do not touch a channel). State-mandated 30-day first-review window (county hits about 25-30 days). Applicant turnaround ranges one week to two months; plat needs can make applicants disappear one to two months; some abandon.
- In city limits the plan goes to the city first, then county; in the ETJ the county approves both. All electronic.
- No zoning in Houston, but platting acts like it (newer plats restrict commercial use; appraisal-district land-use labels are not zoning). Utility and pipeline easements need a "letter of no objection"; pipeline owners are hard to chase.
- Smaller towns between Houston and Dallas/San Antonio can be slow or inconsistent: small staffs, outsourced overloaded engineers (a three-story residential complex took two years in one case).

## Recommended flow (reviewer's view)
1. Engineering site assessment (~$1,500, 1-2 page write-up: jurisdiction, utilities, outfall, floodplain).
2. Architect schematic sketch (a couple thousand dollars).
3. $600 pre-development meeting with county and city before commissioning the full plan set. Underused, mostly by those with means; many owners show up with no engineer or architect.

Illustration of cascading requirements (fast-food restaurant): needs fire sprinklers, so on well water you need a large fire storage tank, which may not fit the site, and a driveway may trigger a left-turn lane from a traffic study (~$500k).

## Signals
- No buy signal. Strong validation that rejections are preventable with pre-submittal checking of review sheets, outfall/detention logic, and plat requirements.
