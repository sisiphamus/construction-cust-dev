# Architect B (K-12 school architecture firm) - budgets blow out every phase; owner-dictated PM platforms and manual documentation

## Workflow by phase
- **Qualifying / selection:** school districts run a process of days or weeks of qualification, interviews and selection before a project starts.
- **Schematic design:** turn the owner's program (a list of rooms or hundreds of spaces with name, square footage and description) into drawings communicating intent. Tools are AutoCAD, with the industry moving to Revit. Some cost estimating happens here to stay within budget.
- **Design development (2-4 months depending on size):** updated design package with more information; requirements differ by client (drawings or a narrative describing the goal).
- **Construction documents (longest phase):** finalized with civil, structural and MEP engineers, with benchmarks at 50% and 75% before final pricing, so the contractor can get a hard number.
- **Bidding (4th phase), then construction administration:** meetings, site visits, photos and field observations to find items to fix; RFIs; contractor submittals where the contractor proposes substituting product X and the architect sometimes pushes back because the client wants Y.
- **Specifications:** a couple hundred pages organized by CSI divisions (about 40), also called the project manual; includes things like temporary trailer power (who pays), insurance, and minimum wage requirements. Often edited from a standard base.

## Budget pain
- At the end of each phase costs are re-evaluated: a third-party estimator hired by the owner, another hired by the architecture firm, and the owner's PM produces an estimate too.
- Most projects go over the owner's budget, depending on year-over-year conditions: tariffs, steel becoming 10-50% more expensive, labor shortages. So there is always a 10% owner contingency ("a very good thing when the owner gets to keep it").
- Example: on a couple of school buildings on one site, the owner added more scope than expected and lacked money, so they redid the building: reduced area, changed materials, usually reduced square footage.
- A construction-manager-at-risk contractor handles about 90% of the budget (pays materials and workers), cannot lose money, and must guarantee the cost; they are hired to manage the project and subs.

## Tools and admin tax
- Project-management platforms are driven by the owner/district PM: Project Mate, Procore (contractors prefer it), Prolog (not used much), and a recent switch to Kahua. The firm must use whichever the client dictates, while the contractor translates between the client's platform and the one used with subs. Clients keep it simple because projects get complicated quickly.
- 20-40 emails a day; platform notifications signal a new submittal or RFI.
- Documentation is vital (the reason for a design decision months later), but meeting minutes and progress photos required by contract are frustrating, repetitive and revised constantly.

## AI opportunity and adoption barrier
An AI-enthusiast contact built 4 apps for the office: a meeting-minutes app (record the conversation, it sorts speakers and outputs a PDF in the office's format; effort drops to 10-15 minutes) and a photo-documentation app (take photos on a device and upload through the app instead of doing it manually). Not adopted: AI concerns and "old way" thinking. The architect notes a human is still needed to check alignment and edit.
