# City Chief Plumbing Inspector A (large Texas city permitting center) - why plumbing jobs fail inspection and how review actually works

Primary-source domain knowledge on plan review and field inspection, relevant to a permitting product. Interviewers: Interviewer 1 and Interviewer 2. A City Plan Analyst Supervisor A was present briefly.

## Key takeaways

**Why jobs fail (the #1 thing):** Not installing per the approved plans. Two recurring causes:

- Field value-engineering: contractors swap a spec'd material for a cheaper or preferred one that is still code-approved. Classic example: the engineer specs copper water piping, the plumber installs PEX because it is cheaper, faster and comes in a roll. It looks fine but is not what the approved plans called for, so it is rejected.
- Bidding off the wrong drawings: plumbers bid off the construction set, not the approved set, so they do not know another material was spec'd. Any deletion of fixtures or change of material requires resubmitting plans for a new approval. They skip it because resubmitting causes delays; the inspector says they never get a good answer for why.

**Codes and the update cycle:** Each code body publishes about every 3 years. The city is on the 2021 code; the bodies already have 2024 and 2027 editions out, so the city is behind and not moving soon. It held the 2012 code for years, then went 2015 to 2021 in about a year.

- Two code bodies: ICC (I-codes, used by Dallas) and IAPMO (Uniform Plumbing/Mechanical Code, used by Houston and likely Austin). ICC publishes nearly everything except electrical (National Electrical Code).
- Interpretation varies by region: soil (Houston digs easily; Austin/San Antonio hit caliche/rock), frost depth (water pipe minimum 12 inches in Houston, deeper in Dallas), and floodplain (in a known flood zone certain appliances, gas meters and backflow preventers must be elevated).
- Code is a legal document: "shall" means must, "may" means discussable. Many compliant ways exist to build the same system if it meets intent.
- Engineered systems: where code does not address something, alternate methods/materials are allowed. The engineer supplies data, a separate group reviews, and the building official signs off.

**Staffing and throughput:** Full staff would be 44 (permit techs, senior field inspectors, one assistant, inspectors). Chronically understaffed because city wages cannot match private (a union plumber vs a city inspector can differ by ~$10/hr; private hours are 60-70/week with on-call, vs 40 in the city). A recent hire left after 4-5 months back to private. Inspectors are in the field 90%+ of the time, averaging ~22-23 inspections a day (peaked at 30 with overtime); the target for a quality inspection is 15. Pre-COVID, better staffed, they ran 650-700 per day across the office. All inspections are cleared same-day. About 70% of inspectors are certified, but certification does not equal knowledge.

**How plumbers actually learn code (red-tag education):** Most field plumbers do not own or read the code book. When a job fails, the inspector leaves a red tag listing deficiencies; that is how the plumber learns. Inspectors get name-dropped ("the chief said I could do this") to pressure younger inspectors into passing. The chief's standard: "I don't want to know what you think or feel, I want to know what you know" against the book. It takes 2-5 years to make a good inspector.

**Inspector consistency:** Variation in leniency is about personality, not experience. "Labrador" types want to be liked and pass things they maybe should not; "new sheriff" types over-enforce on personal preference. Neither is right; the book is the standard.

**Why the rules exist (life safety):** Cross-connection/backflow can contaminate potable water (a garden hose in a fertilized puddle siphoned back during a main break; a kinked toilet fill tube drawing cleaning-tablet water into an ice maker; a mis-installed water softener in another Texas city that vacuumed sewer water into drinking supply and forced a boil notice for an area).

**Tech/process changes:** 30 years ago everything was pen and paper; over ~20 years Palm Pilots, phones, then tablets. Paper plans moved to all-digital submittals; inspectors and office both access plans in the field. A recent mayoral initiative targets residential plans out in 30 days. Residential gets lighter review (mostly architectural soundness), so the inspector must field-verify sizing, because residential is not spelled out like commercial (where drawings specify pipe sizes at each point).

**Complexity by job type:** Small jobs (lease spaces, outdoor kitchens, residential additions) are simple. Large ones (medical center, high/mid-rise, hospitals) get complicated: engineers miss overlays (plumbing running into mechanical), beams not accounted for, and tight deadlines push contractors to request partial releases so they can occupy and draw funds ("it's always about the money"). Medical gas needs a separate state endorsement and a different code. Inspection sequence on a new build: groundwork (all plumbing inside the footprint, grease vs waste lines separate until the grease trap, trap primers on floor drains), then rough-in (in-wall drainage, waste, venting, traps, fire caulking at penetrations), then finals.

**Plan reviewers vs inspectors:** The city uses specialized reviewers per trade. The chief inspector is the go-to liaison for plan review and occupancy (a separate group inspects already-occupied commercial sites for unpermitted alterations; every business needs a certificate of occupancy). Questions flow both ways to keep uniformity.

**Expediters / permit runners:** Some work closely with the office, especially engineers trying to pass plans first time (plan review deals with them more than inspections). Others act as permit runners/middlemen chasing finals for contractors.

**Integrity:** Bribery is rare but real. This year one inspector was recorded soliciting a $1,000 bribe; over 32 years only a handful of cases. Random drug tests; refusal or a positive means termination. Staff cannot accept gifts such as donuts; they must go in the break room.

**Where to learn more:** The city permitting center's public website lists the start-to-finish process and required documents (for example, what is needed to install a water meter). Both ICC I-codes and IAPMO UPC are readable online, plus local amendments. Every city differs procedurally but shares a base code plus municipal amendments.
