# Developer A (Houston multi-segment developer) - permitting is manageable, inconsistency across cities is the pain; jobsite software must be dead simple

Interviewers: Interviewer 1 (lead) and Interviewer 2. Call length about 15 minutes.

## Market and strategy
- Betting on industrial/warehouse (first warehouse ~26 years ago, dedicated industrial arm from 2020) because of the e-commerce trend and robotics over the next ten years; "all these robots need a place to work".
- Modular/prefab housing: many have tried, including him, and it has not worked. Labor is cheap in Texas; prefab homes have turned out more expensive than building on site. Better odds in the Northeast and West Coast.
- Labor: "the biggest problem in this country, no one wants to work", especially young people. Robotics could re-shore outsourced jobs.
- Current market: industrial still holding up; apartments very slow; residential slow; commercial "here and there".
- Does not use market-data aggregators or consultants ("old school", figures it out himself). Very positive on AI ("beyond good") and uses it in his own software.

## Permitting
- "The problem is every municipality is different." Everyone interprets code a little differently and has a different submission process.
- Example: a city in the Dallas area would not allow an elevator shaft in a large townhouse project, while every other jurisdiction where he built townhouses did. A Houston-area suburb he is working in applies "mind-boggling" scrutiny.
- He wasted roughly 4-5 months waiting on that suburb for a preliminary plat; wanted comments, approval of the preliminary plan, and finish the rest at final plan, but that is not how they work. Hundreds of emails on planning alone, even though the city was supportive and admits its process is crazy.
- Everyone knows the code; the issue is local interpretation and requirements, not code knowledge or aggregation.
- Ask: something like a "common application" (as in college admissions) across cities. "So far no one has come up with anything like that."
- Not familiar with permit-automation software. "Permitting is not a huge problem for us here in Texas... four months delay, five months delay, but not two years. It's manageable."

## His own software
- Uses systems built in-house over 20-25 years, with AI added in the last 2-3 years. Two products: (1) payment/AP automation ("take over your whole accounts payable department"); (2) real-time jobsite photo tracking with counting (for example, a sheetrock delivery is photographed and counted so a short delivery is caught), delivered through a chat-based interface, with patents.
- Builds residential and some commercial in house; on larger industrial projects investors will not allow in-house systems.
- Selling it externally now. Users like it and suggest tweaks, but he is "not making a lot of progress" and needs to get better at selling. Spent roughly $4.5M so far, mostly on polish and user experience, and is "not setting the world on fire".
- Philosophy: jobsite workers are largely lower-education and will not spend five minutes learning; the system must need no training. Enterprise software (SAP and the like) requires schooling and consultants. Basic tier is simple, with upgrade tiers available. He found no comparable product on the market ("there's nothing quite like that").
- Interviewers echoed that contractors they spoke with found a competing product "too complicated, too many offerings." He: "That's the key."

## Interviewer ideas floated
- Aggregating permit outcomes and approvals by municipality so no one has to guess policy; a developer asking for pipeline/pricing data aggregation; a firm wanting marketing channels consolidated. Interviewers were mainly interested in construction.

## Close
He offered to help and to meet again if they have something to show. Interviewer 2 mentioned a family background in HVAC/electrical subcontracting; the developer suggested that could mean interest in his software.
