# Expediter A (14-year Austin permit expediter) - code knowledge plus reviewer relationships, residential

## Who and business
- 14 years expediting in Austin; no formal technical education, learned on the job. Interviewer 1 reached out as a university student doing customer-dev, referencing a developer's talk.
- Started as a "glorified courier" when plans had to be printed and walked into the city and GCs/architects had no time. Watching rejections and architects' fixes taught him code; now consults ("the city rejected for this reason, here's how to fix").
- Now a permit + design + engineering shop: brokers a drafter and a structural engineer (origin: a pool builder with bad plans; he knew a guy, split the fee, got first-time approval, then advertised it).
- 95% residential; turns away commercial ("its own beast", requires deep electrical, mechanical, plumbing). Currently doing a consult for a large homebuilder on ~270 homes ("we are their permit monkeys").
- Client mix by volume: GCs first, architecture firms second, homeowners third.

## Why projects get kicked back
- #1 reason: code non-compliance, not missing documents.
- Austin traps: 40% lot coverage cap (site plan and coverage calcs; e.g. 10,000 sqft lot = 4,000 max); protected trees over ~19 in. (no digging near them, show protection); pool rules (enclosure incl. fencing front yard, electrical bonding, door/window alarms into the enclosure); detached accessory structures under 200 sqft need no permit but above that trigger full 2024 IRC with engineered foundation, framing, lateral bracing; garage cannot face more forward than the building front and, if attached, must be offset back ~20% of building width; hillside lakefront zoning that counts steep slopes as zero buildable area.
- His own projects get kicked back when a builder goes cheap and hires an out-of-town submitter unfamiliar with local rules, or a homeowner bought a house with prior unpermitted work.

## Code versus people
- Value is both code knowledge and reviewer relationships. Reviewers prefer submitters who know code; with his name on an application they are more inclined to move it because he back-checks his work and makes their job easier.
- Old days: in-person, candy-bar relationship greasing. Now mostly digital (portal, email, occasional virtual meeting); reviewers turn over, but digital relationships build over repeat dealings. No tricks beyond clean, well-formatted, code-correct documents.
- Code is public but "knowing what to look for is the hard part"; he built it over years of rejections.
- Loopholes he uses: fire-rate the garage (20-minute self-closing door) to isolate its area and drop the house under the 3,600 sqft threshold that otherwise requires a hydrant within 600 ft at 1,750 GPM or a sprinkler system; Texas Chapter 245 vested rights to grandfather a lot to its platting date and defeat later restrictive zoning (works mainly in high-red-tape jurisdictions).

## Geography
- Website lists Austin, Dallas, Fort Worth but is outdated. The DFW expansion failed: cold calling with no digital marketing, and DFW has little red tape so expediting is worth less. Austin is the breadwinner and they are slammed. Has not tried Houston (no zoning, "weird" restrictions, no bandwidth).

## Interviewer read
- Strong supply-side confirmation of the permitting thesis: moat = jurisdiction-specific code traps plus trusted reviewer relationships, both of which software could encode.
- Pain and willingness to pay concentrate in high-red-tape jurisdictions (Austin yes, DFW no, Houston unclear), suggesting the easiest market may have the least pain.
- He is a knowledge source and intro node, not a prospect.
