# GC Preconstruction Lead (Houston multifamily GC) - water meter / city legal is a hidden permitting track

## Summary

Recorded at a Houston multifamily conference run by a commercial real estate media company. Two parts. First, a hallway conversation with a GC preconstruction/permitting lead, standing with GC Project Manager A and GC Estimator A, about why multifamily permitting drags. His point: most of what causes delays could be cleared on the front side before the city approves the project, but the city does not require it until later. The standout example is the water meter / water account. It runs through a separate "city legal" process (6-8 weeks) on top of the permitting office, involving deeds, metes and bounds, and a survey, all of which could have been done and approved before the job permit was issued. He notes a long-tenured staffer (~15 years) in the permit office who ironed out a lot for him, and that every person they talk to names a new department. The second part is a stage presentation by a cost-segregation vendor rep on accelerated depreciation (general tax background, not central). A panel on leasing/management/operations is summarized at the end.

## Roles in the conversation

- GC Preconstruction Lead A: gets work into the house to bid; "a lot of my roles are more preconstruction"; says he spends time "putting fires out after".
- GC Project Manager A: makes sure the work gets built.
- GC Estimator A: makes sure it is bid properly.
- Interviewer 1 and Interviewer 2: Rice students in a summer innovation program, building and piloting software with a Houston developer to expedite permitting.

## Key takeaways

**Permitting (the relevant part):**
- The preconstruction lead said much of the friction could be handled up front, before the city approves the project: "Once they give that permit, there should be no more questions... once you get stamped drawings, there should be no more."
- **Water meter / water account is a separate, slow track.** Setting up the water account goes through "city legal" and takes about 6-8 weeks, separate from the permitting office and city hall. It involves many legal documents (deeds, metes and bounds, survey). In multifamily the land owner is often not the apartment owner (ground lease), which adds paperwork. He argues all of it could be cleared before the job permit is issued if a water meter is already on the stamped/approved plans, but the city does not require it until you go to pull the meter. That forces return trips and delay; you are "almost closing the house" before you can put the meter in.
- **Fragmentation:** "every person we talk to mentions a new department." One long-tenured staffer in the permit office (~15 years) "knows" and ironed out a lot; he still had to track down ~5 different people.
- Reinforces the recurring theme across these interviews: you think you pull a permit and you are good, but it is a roller coaster of departments and can stretch ~3 months. "It all depends."
- Interviewer 2 asked what he currently does to get around it; the answer was not captured in the recording.

**Conference framing:** The organizer publishes commercial real estate magazines and runs ~60 events a year. Useful as a venue full of Houston multifamily owners, GCs and vendors.

**Cost segregation talk (background, not core):** A vendor rep pitched cost segregation: an IRS-approved depreciation method that buckets short-life items (5/7/15-year) instead of straight-line over 27.5/39 years, freeing cash flow early. Apartment complexes typically have ~30% short-life items (30-40% in general). 100% bonus depreciation is back retroactively for property acquired after 01/19/2025 (it had phased down: 80% for 2023, 60% for 2024). One-time catch-up study for earlier purchases via a change-of-accounting-method form, no approval needed. Example: $1M depreciable basis, ~30% short-life gives ~$300K extra first-year depreciation, roughly $100K+ tax savings. Study takes ~60 days; the vendor cited 15,000+ studies, questioned by the IRS 9 times with no line-item changes; uses appraisers rather than engineers to keep cost down.

## Leasing / management / operations panel (background)

Panelists were operators and a construction director from several Houston-area multifamily firms, plus a moderator from a vendor.

**State of the market:** Houston moves as submarkets, not one city. Supply is slowing; recovery submarket by submarket, likely late 2026 to early 2027 before stabilized occupancy (~93%) returns. Concession-driven market; stabilized properties running 11-12% vacancy. Refinancing at roughly double the prior cycle's rates. One 30-year operator is not ready to call the bottom; workforce housing is hit hardest as renters double up or leave. Qualified traffic is declining; more rejections than acceptances. Execution gaps covered up in the 2021-22 run-up are now visible.

**Customer service as the key differentiator:** Property management is a people business and customer service has been underinvested. The "must-have amenity" for the next year is inspired customer service, not a new amenity. Empower staff as business owners; measure NOI, not just occupancy.

**Innovations the panel thinks should be adopted:**
- Requiring delinquent residents to sit down with the manager for a financial analysis; found renters spending $1,000+/month on rideshare unaware it was the problem. Financial literacy as retention tool.
- Early AI/tech adoption to cut redundant tasks and free staff for face time. Counterpoint from another panelist: technology often adds screen time rather than freeing it; the "more time for customers" pitch rarely plays out.
- One operator leased 62 units with zero concessions by shifting brand identity from concession-giver to experience-giver.

**Empowering staff:** mentoring, peer support groups, training for the whole company, associate engagement scores tracked like resident satisfaction, visible growth paths. Quote: "If you're putting a camera in the office to watch what they're doing, you're way lost."

Note: the panel is background on the multifamily operator community, not directly relevant to the permitting question.
