# Developer A (Texas/Florida retail developer) - permitting is THE problem, offered to be first customer

## Bottom line

Permitting is the problem. Developer A said it ~90 times and explicitly told the interviewers to drop cost/supply/material and build only an AI permit solution. He has been evangelizing this exact idea for a while ("on my soapbox with a megaphone"). He offered to be the first customer. His analysis implies the tool may be better aimed at municipalities (reviewing submissions faster) or architects (vetting plans before submission) than at developers paying for a pre-submission tool.

## Who he is

- President of a retail development firm. Convenient-service retail (5-55 acre sites, 15k-45k sqft centers, mom-and-pop anchored). Not malls, power centers or grocery-anchored centers.
- ~29 active projects across Texas and Florida; developed in 7 states over 4 decades; manages ~165 shopping centers. Runs projects around $60M.
- Connected to the interviewers through a university entrepreneurship program. Credible, deep, blunt. A real buyer persona, not a tire-kicker.

## The product he wants (his words)

1. Ingest all permitting data and guidelines for a city, then a whole state.
2. Developer registers online, fills out the normal info, submits plans.
3. About 20 minutes later AI has vetted the plans against every formal guideline and kicks back redlines. Fix and resubmit. Within a day you are AI-passed (phase 1).
4. Phase 2: a human at the city reviews practical items not in the guidelines (curb cuts, median cuts, traffic flow). Days to weeks, not 6-24 months.
5. Charge a fee. Separate residential and commercial buckets to go faster.
6. Centralize all municipalities in a region so a submission to one city also flags ancillary items common across the metro.

He noted AI would vet far beyond what a human catches and eliminates nobody's job (kills the fear objection).

## Why permitting and not cost/supply

- Permitting timelines: 6-10 months typical; one Round Rock-area pad (quick-service restaurant, coffee, retail) is ~22-24 months in and still not permitted, despite leases signed and plans done. The hold-up was special documents signed by an adjacent school, then the city attorney, then filed of record.
- Every ripple comes back to permitting: lost city tax revenue; lender interest not accruing on a 30-month interest-only construction loan; equity-partner return curve flattening (IRR drops even though dollars do not change); tenant build-out delays cascading back to the landlord. The bank on that Round Rock-area loan (~$10M at ~7.75-8%) asked whether to pull the plug and restart.
- "Schedule slippage / material delays" that other interviewees raised is "a code word for permits."
- On cost/supply: not affecting them. They built five apartment projects last year with no supply problem. When lumber spiked (~$600 to ~$1,800) they hedged by sequencing (underground utilities and an off-site precast parking garage first), told the lender they would not need lumber for 8 months, and saved ~$750-800K when prices fell. "Cost is going to be cost." On a $60M project it does not move the needle.

## War stories

- Every municipality starts from scratch; track record does not help; it gets "personal, not professional." Staff are overworked and understaffed and many came from other markets with different guidelines.
- His firm uses a tactic: bring red pins, throw plans on the desk and ask staff to mark them up, so they avoid the 6-8 month resubmission game. Still get kicked out two or three months at a time.
- After permit, a whole new group of inspectors arrives. An inspector says move the drain; if you do not adhere he can shut you down: "an ego trap."
- A Florida multi-tenant retail project: ~8 months ping-ponging in a permit department until the city admitted the developer was right and treated it as two separate developments.
- A Dallas-suburb project: budgeted ~$150K in permit fees for two 15K sqft centers and two pads; plans came back at ~$250K per building plus ~$250K per out-parcel. A law firm found the city's formula; the city said it would not know until it issued a permit. Had they known, they would have submitted as one building. "Who would ever think to ask how bad you're going to screw me on permit fees?"
- Arbitrary asks not in any code: brick on all sides, a $400K circular school-bus drive at the front of an apartment project that is mostly one-bedrooms with no families, a 360-degree glass building, a preferred building color in another state, and an 18-month-plus council-agenda delay. Another city refused 3-story apartments because it had no fire truck that reaches the third floor; he offered to buy a $250K fire truck and was refused. Years later a 3-story was built there because the city realized it was losing tax revenue to a neighboring jurisdiction.
- Expediters: "window dressing," thousands of dollars, does not move the needle.
- Other states: Hawaii 3-4 years; Colorado ~24 months for entitlements before permitting starts ("rezoning, might as well call it 10 years"); Florida not bad; Nevada unions; coasts worse than Texas.
- Tenant permits, sign permits, landscaping permits are all additional layers. A city may refuse a tenant's build-out permit until the landlord gets a temporary or final certificate of occupancy (some demand permanent C of O because developers skipped landscaping; fix is a bond/escrow). The tenant (160-180 days before rent starts) cannot build in time, loses money, and comes back to the landlord, who eats months of lost rent and CAM.
- Growing exurban cities often have no mature guidelines of their own and borrow pieces from neighbors, so requirements differ city to city (e.g., one requires a "360" building, a neighbor allows a traditional strip center).

## The hard part (his own caveat)

Much of the pain is people, not guidelines: inspectors flexing ego, arbitrary asks not in any code. AI cleanly solves the guidelines-vetting half; the personal/political half is harder.

## Interviewer follow-up (early June)

- The interviewers drafted an email with a demo video (one-button upload, vet, move forward) and a new data point: the head of a large Texas city's permitting office said, in effect, "we approve permits at light speed," totally unlike his experience. The email also asked: when a project stalls, is it from things you did not see coming, or do you know what is needed and it just drags?

## Second session (mid-June, whole student cohort)

- Interviewers told the cohort they started with the thesis that material cost was #1, but a week of visits to a city permitting office, planning office, a county, and cities along the Houston-Dallas corridor changed that. They built a code-tracking demo, but at a developer breakfast realized the real gap is the specific rules individual municipalities fixate on, which are hard to find without meetings and real case review. They have also been filing public records requests for municipal code data; one city outside Frisco replied "yes, please give us that information."
- Developer A's firm never goes into a municipality cold: they contact the city before submitting and often meet face to face. Architects and engineers know the people. It still drags.
- A fast-growing city south of Houston: ~14 months of "boxing" with a city; every council meeting postponed; ~$2M in expenses, hard contracts with equity, debt and tenants idle; individual council members added personal demands (the circular driveway); they had to hire a local attorney with community ties. His message to municipalities: "Think about how much tax revenue you have lost. That $80M project could have been built in 14 months... on your tax rolls within 18 months."
- Observation he made: every person the interviewers spoke to showed a stress response ("blood pressure") when permitting came up and began listing cities they hate dealing with. His verdict: "It's a space that is needed."
- Refined idea: growing municipalities that lack mature guidelines could overlay their codes against mature neighbors (without paying consultants). He endorsed the data-collection approach: "data is the most powerful commodity right now."
- Offered to connect the interviewers with architects in his network.
- Earlier fragment: if he knew permitting delay dates upfront he could time construction-loan drawdown closer to actual build start, saving months of interest; relevant to how he presents timeline slippage to investors.
- Coaching theme to all teams in the room: think about distribution before customers; go to the big channel players, not door-to-door. (Other student teams pitched unrelated ideas; not relevant here.)

## Tenant-mix context

- Ladder of retail: malls (nobody builds them), power centers (100+ acres, big boxes), grocery-anchor, then his tier built on mom-and-pop tenants. In 2020, of ~165 centers only 6 tenants went out; 3 backfilled in under a month. National chains sent form letters demanding 25% rent cuts and were ignored.
- Bigger developers feel permitting too, just with less time sensitivity (15-year horizons on 100+ acre sites).
