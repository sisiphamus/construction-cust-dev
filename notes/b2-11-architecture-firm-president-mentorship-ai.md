# Architect President A (Midwest architecture firm) - prestige vs practical skills, AI adoption, licensing avoidance

Participants: Interviewer 2, the president, a student interviewer, and another architect, at an architecture conference. About 20 minutes.

## Key points

**Prestige vs practical skills.** Skilled tradespeople (electricians, welders) can reportedly earn $500k-$1M+/year (some own multiple companies) but get no interest from university students, who want lawyer/banker/doctor roles. Teaching is devalued in the US (teacher shortage) vs parts of Southeast Asia where prestige means no shortage. Trades can offer retire-early paths, while white-collar paths carry debt and 30+ year horizons. "We don't need more lawyers. We need more plumbers."

**Compressed timelines.** Students file 15-20+ college applications (vs 3-5 before), early recruiting pushes internships freshman year, and social media creates constant "next milestone" pressure. Little time to ask what is fulfilling.

**Fear of failure in architecture firms.** Young architects will not take licensing exams even with required experience hours completed. Root cause: failure feels like loss of status. A 10% raise offer did not move them. The firm's new program pays architects to study (about 3 hrs/week paid plus 6 hrs personal study required, and they must schedule the exam). Quote: "What do they call the guy who graduated last in medical school? Doctor." (licensing is a gate, not a measure of excellence).

**AI adoption in firms.**
- Older architects are the most eager to adopt AI; emerging architects are slower, possibly from automation anxiety.
- The president uses AI 3-4 times a day but non-orchestrated and wants a strategic approach.
- Opportunity: strategic AI implementation, but human resistance must be solved first.

**Mentorship.** Influencer "mentors" are not real mentors. Advice: find mentors in each age bracket (20s, 30s, 40s) and learn vicariously. Book recommendation: The Medici Effect (innovation from cross-disciplinary work).

**Interviewers' angle.** An interviewer is building approval-optimization software with Houston developers using data from codes, reviewers and offices. Strategy: show up at unfamiliar conferences, talk to people, learn faster than any article. A quote from the student: "If you could live at the ninetieth percentile, you'd be a millionaire."

## Notes

Cross-disciplinary approach may yield novel solutions; it was unusual for a partner of that firm to attend the conference, flying in specifically to gather information.
