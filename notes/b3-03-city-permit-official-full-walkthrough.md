# City Permitting Official A (large Texas city permitting center) - full permitting process walkthrough

Interviewers visited the office in person. The official came up through the field, was a plan reviewer, ran the plan reviewers' office, is technically retired from the city but still in a senior role. He runs the city's pre-development meetings, is bilingual and personally conducts meetings in Spanish for foreign developers. High-value insider for the permitting bet.

## The full high-rise path

**1. Replat + Planning Commission (~5-6 months).** Developers often buy land made of multiple recorded tracts and must combine them into one (a replat) through the city planning department. A replat triggers a public notice and a Planning Commission hearing. This is essentially the ONLY point where the public can object to a project, and even then the commission only considers removing recorded lot lines, not what is built. High rises and gas stations draw pushback (gas stations because of the vapor recovery riser on the canopy; high rises because nobody wants one next door). Plan on ~6 months with a city planner and civil engineers.

**2. Concurrent reviews.** Water: apply for a water letter confirming infrastructure capacity AND available capacity for the new units. Traffic: submit an access application; the traffic department assesses trips in/out, which usually triggers a traffic study. These run in parallel with the main submittal.

**3. Plan review (~15 business days per cycle, ~4 cycles, ~6 months).** Standard is 15 business days regardless of size (a doctor's office and a 30-story high rise get the same clock). Expect ~4 cycles, ~60 business days, roughly 6 months. Intake routes the project to every department that must review: electrical, mechanical, plumbing, fire marshal, water, structural, signs, etc., up to 13-15 departments. A dashboard count of ~2,000 projects really means ~10x that many individual reviews. The city uses SPECIALIZED reviewers per trade (many from the field) rather than the single "four-hat" reviewer/inspector model smaller jurisdictions use. In Texas plumbers must be state-tested and licensed, so plumbing is its own discipline.

**4. Fees.** Plan review is billed as a 25% deposit up front, remaining 75% due on approval. It is a deposit, not an extra fee, and charged only once even with multiple rejections.

**5. Contractor registration.** GC, electrician, plumber, mechanical must all be registered with the city.

**6. Inspection phase + phasing.** Projects can be split into multiple permits: horizontal/sitework (underground utilities, paving), then foundation/piers (10-50 ft deep) separately, then superstructure; subterranean parking can be foundation-to-grade then superstructure. Phasing keeps the field on schedule but the risk is GCs calling inspections on the wrong project number when many are open. His advice for a high rise: open the FEWEST project numbers you can. Inspections are NEXT-DAY: schedule before 2:30pm and the inspector is there tomorrow.

**7. CO / TCO.** A Certificate of Occupancy is required to occupy (a driver's license for the building; plenty operate without one until caught). Developers often want to occupy before finish, which means a Temporary CO. For a TCO the exiting elements must be in place: stairs, shafts, fire protection/suppression; the goal is everyone can safely exit before releasing floors.

## Why the city is fast (the edge)

- **Electronic plan review via ProjectDox.** Moved from paper to online around pre-COVID; files live in the system, so they never missed a beat during COVID. Controlled launch with architect friends trying to break it; the first online permit issued was a cell tower for an out-of-state user who found the not-yet-advertised system.
- **Pre-development meetings.** Offered here; New York and Chicago do NOT offer them. He runs them "selfishly": educating the developer before drawings makes both sides faster. He triages by questions asked, pulls only relevant departments, or routes a simple question to the right person instead of making them wait two weeks for a formal meeting.
- **Speed vs peers.** NY permits per borough (high rises route to a central hub; fire districts add complexity); NY inspections take 1-2 weeks. A California team faced 6-8 months for a traffic variance that this city turns in 4-6 weeks. The city has historically led the country in single-family permits.

## Local specifics / "backdoor zoning"

- **No zoning.** But de facto "backdoor zoning" via specific permits, e.g. a liquor store cannot sit within ~1,000 ft of a church or daycare (handled by a separate regulatory department). A downtown stadium vs church alcohol-sales dispute needed government involvement.
- **Noise / hours.** No hours-of-operation ordinance, so work can legally run at 2am, BUT there are decibel limits. Foundation pours happen in the wee hours (a monolithic pour cannot dry), with ~300 concrete trucks staging, generating 311 noise/light complaints. Exceeding decibel limits (festivals, concerts) requires a noise permit.
- **Flood.** Flood review runs through FEMA, so rules are rigid with little discretion. A 100/500-year floodplain is a frequent deal-breaker; a floodway is essentially un-buildable; bringing in fill requires mitigation.

## What causes the long delays (his answer)

- **Public/political opposition (rare).** A high rise near a university area was locked ~2 years by public and political pressure; a new developer later bought the permit and restarted reviews. In a cemetery-discovery case, neighbors flagged a long-gone cemetery, it was confirmed, the developer replatted around the graves and returned to the Planning Commission; neighbors still fought it.
- **Funding drying up.** Developer A runs out of money, Developer B buys the permit and continues.
- **Permit validity.** A permit is good for 180 days; planning can extend once for another 180. In the field, every inspection auto-extends the permit 180 days from the inspection date.
- **A failed inspection does NOT bounce you back to plan review.** Only a revision for that specific element (a wall moved, a substituted window product). Stamped plans are legal documents, archived ~5 years.
- **Big projects are just long.** Medical-center hospital towers run 2+ years because of equipment assessment. Stadium/major-event related work is active right now.

## Self-certification (product-relevant)

Architects self-certifying that a drawing complies with code. The city does NOT allow it for foundations (cannot see the rebar). San Antonio has a limited self-cert program, only some residential projects and certain elements. He was skeptical of the city sending a large staff group to study it ("milkshake drive-through window" analogy: no separate fast lane for one small review). New York requires a pre-construction meeting with the building department before any inspections can be scheduled; this city lacks the resources.

## Market read (mid-2026)

Construction is slow: interest rates, political climate and a war are making developers hold funds. Residential way down: ~275 houses in the system now vs a peak north of 1,000; overall project counts have been as high as ~2,000-4,000. Solar is the growth area, with a dedicated review team.

## Pre-development vs due-diligence meetings

- **Pre-development:** developer is committed and scouting locations (e.g. a drive-through coffee chain taking over former fast-casual sites that already have a grease trap and interceptor).
- **Due-diligence:** many what-ifs; project may not happen. He still meets them and surfaces deal-breakers early: floodplain, water capacity/availability (you may think you can tie into your own right-of-way but actually must bore down another street, blowing the budget), and sign restrictions (a burger chain's site focused on sign count and height; an overpass can block a sign at the allowed height).

## Staffing, pay, and why people leave

- People leave managers, not organizations. At the city you inherit a team and cannot pick people. Old-timers stay until retirement; younger people use the city as a training stepping stone. Consultant firms regularly try to recruit him.
- **Pay gap is large.** A city plan reviewer starting ~$70k can do the same job for a third-party reviewer/consultant starting ~$107k.
- **No real training program; knowledge moves by osmosis.** New reviewers sit next to old-timers with the code books open (he keeps the 2021 code, an old 1994 UBC, even a 1972 code on his desk for cross-referencing). New plan examiners shadow an inspector first; new inspectors rotate through plan review. Schools do not teach building code. Where a review starts depends on discipline: structural at the site plan, electrical at the one-line diagram / panel schedule, mechanical at air changes / thermostats.
- **Pressure is from production volume, not life-safety,** and flows down politically: complaints reach the mayor's office and come back down ("why has this been six months" when he only saw it yesterday). Reviewers are under immense pressure; some leave for lower-stress design-side jobs. Active crunch right now on stadium and major-event projects.

## Longest / hardest reviews

Structural is the most time-consuming. On the civil side, traffic takes longest. The office is split into "protein" teams (electrical, mechanical, plumbing) and civil teams (detention, traffic studies, right-of-way).

## Traffic as a deal-breaker

Traffic approval can force a left or right turn lane, often not in the budget, which can kill a project. A small tenant adding a driveway is one thing; a restaurant that generates more trips can trigger a required turn lane. Operators will not fund city infrastructure they cannot recoup if they close in a few years.

## Airport tiers (a hidden deal-breaker)

The city has three airports, each with three concentric noise tiers (Tier 1 closest). Tier requirements add sound attenuation: specific windows, doors and wall assemblies. Real example: a developer spent ~$30-50k on drawings and ~6-8 months in planning for an apartment complex, only to learn at structural review that the site was in Tier 1 and apartments could NOT be built there. Nobody had told him earlier.

## The non-linear process / no early feasibility gate (KEY for the product)

Interviewers pushed directly: platting, airport-tier checks, flood, water capacity and structural review happen in parallel or late, so a developer can burn months and tens of thousands of dollars before learning the building cannot be built on that site. The official agreed: "everybody dropped the ball, never told this guy." There is no upfront feasibility gate. This is a concrete, repeated pain and a direct opening for a fast upfront feasibility/eligibility check.

## Multifamily performance standard review

Apartments trigger a "performance standard review" pulling in the fire marshal, who checks fire access. The city uses fire access roads (so the largest apparatus can set up) rather than painted red fire lanes common in the Northeast.

## Why the city is faster than smaller fast-growing towns

Interviewers raised that developers along the Houston-to-Dallas corridor hit permitting and inspection delays of months to years in small towns; one large developer was losing ~$100k/day during a two-week stretch. His read:

- Volume-driven efficiency: more reps and years of trial and error.
- Dedicated, separate teams for review vs inspection. Small towns often use one "four-hat" person for both; he doubts one person can hold that depth across trades.
- Next-day and after-hours/overtime inspections (e.g. a 2am foundation pour) that smaller cities do not offer.
- He thinks delays in other cities lean more on the INSPECTION side than review.
- Small-city quirk: a tree-protection ordinance can freeze a project until resolved.
- Inspectors are assigned to fixed zones, same electrical/mechanical/plumbing inspector per zone, so they know active projects nearby. City cars carry GPS trackers.

## Office of City Engineer (separate concurrent review)

Connecting to the right-of-way (water/sewer, a plan and profile) goes to the Office of City Engineer, a separate review on its own timeline, run concurrently with the building-code review. Another fragmented entity.

## County vs city + a referral

The county is "a whole different animal." Historically it did only a fire-code review plus civil, no building code; it has added more oversight recently but is still lighter. His view: it is easier to build a daycare in the county, but the city's many reviews mean many eyes, which he equates to safety. **Referral:** he offered to introduce the interviewers to the chief engineer at the county (a former acting director at the city). Accepted; follow up by email.

## Design phase + architects want code pre-review (product-relevant)

High-rise design takes 1-2 years before the city ever sees drawings, so the 15-day review is a small slice. Key signal: because architecture programs do NOT teach building code, designers actively want a third party to review their drawings against code before they stamp them. Texas law also requires schools to be reviewed by a municipality that enforces building code; a rural school with no local oversight cannot be accredited unless it gets a code review. This is a demand signal for an AI code-check aimed at architects/designers, not just developers.

## Misc

- "The building outlasts the review": review time is crumbs versus a building standing 50-100 years. A themed aquarium restaurant took ~8-10 months to permit (jungle finishes triggered flame-spread/smoke-index checks, live animals required zoo qualification, a rooftop helipad drew FAA objections).
- How the interviewers framed themselves: interest in why cities and companies grow and scale, tied to a university entrepreneurship center; they had recently spoken with the president of a large Houston developer and want to help that team with small-city permitting timelines.
