# Vendor A (Singapore data-center cooling software) - advise, don't decide; Singapore as a market

## Key takeaways

- **Advise, don't decide (load-bearing principle).** The vendor's software flags inefficiencies and recommends set points but does not auto-change them. Full autonomy would need an extremely robust (expensive) system and shifts liability to whoever turns it on (the vendor), driving price way up. Operators are not trained to take that on, and since most downtime is human error, the mindset is never to touch running equipment. Interviewer 2 mapped this to a mentor's advice for the permitting product: if you spot a plan out of code, or someone asks whether they can be 22 ft from the curb instead of 25 ft, do not fix it or answer yes/no; only recommend, so if wrong you advised rather than decided. The contact agreed, saying the same applies in his company.
- **Singapore market is structurally different from Houston.** Mostly large condo developments (one permit can cover hundreds to ~1,000 units) and government infrastructure; private market is small with few projects, so contractors always bid low. Migrant labor from Southeast/Greater Asia keeps costs down (largely documented now). Land is scarce and population aging, so the outlook pushed the contact out of construction into software.
- **Why Singapore could still matter.** He thinks a Houston permitting product could adapt (permitting has its own problems there), capital is looking to deploy, and Singapore is where multinationals headquarter Southeast Asia operations, making it convenient to sell or raise. A US VC backer would likely also raise there. Possible models: US office plus a Singapore office, a reseller, or him selling on the interviewer's behalf.
- **Data-center cooling business.** Data centers are designed for peak IT load but run far below it for the first years, wasting energy cooling absent servers. The software pulls from the building management system, trains AI to find set points matched to current load, saving owners ~hundreds of thousands of dollars per year. Sweet spot is data centers ~4-6 years old (newer already efficient; much older ones retrofit hardware). Adoption friction is the mindset change and added operational risk of touching set points. Operators focus on uptime and set equipment to effective-not-efficient settings, never touching them again.
- **Interviewer's pitch ideas (on the call).** In Houston, ~350,000 single-family permits per year; trades are saturated, so bidding wars drive prices down. Idea: use analytics to reach homeowners at the moment of need (like injury insurers contacting accident victims early), e.g. contractors targeting families expecting a newborn who will expand a house. The contact said the same pattern would apply in Singapore, where each child gets high investment. Also noted: immigration enforcement news pauses Houston construction for weeks.
- **Anchor customer signal cited by Interviewer 2.** A Houston real-estate firm president of ~45 years said "permitting" ~90 times in an hour and offered to be the first customer if the interviewers build expediting software.
