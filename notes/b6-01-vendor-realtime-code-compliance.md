# Vendor Founder A (3-person architecture-software startup) - real-time compliance, human stamp stays

## Context
Interviewer 2 spoke with Vendor Founder A, CEO and co-founder of a small bootstrapped startup building real-time code compliance for the design phase. He is a trained architect (worked in a large European firm, "reached the top of the pyramid" in about three years), and he and two colleagues left when firm leadership refused to give them equity in an internal product-business. The product started as their own scripts (Rhino, Grasshopper, Revit, Dynamo) used for urban feasibility studies and competitions where work is repetitive.

## Problem he is solving
- Early-phase competition decisions are made on intuition: place volumes, looks good, submit. Firms won competitions and then found "nothing of this is buildable" once they checked requirements/compliance.
- Competition economics: architects work for free or over a fixed sum, everyone works over hours to beat the average, so nobody can check everything. Focus goes to illustrations and facades; numbers get a token pass. Diving into the national code plus site-specific regulation takes time and the goal at that stage is "seducing the client."
- Existing solutions: all post-design (a Revit plug-in or an AI PDF checker). Competitors named: OpenCodes, CodeComply (Interviewer 2 had seen them at the AIA expo in San Diego).
- His differentiation: "we are the spell checker for architects." If you cap stories at five and draw seven, it flags it in the scene and in a list immediately, not a week later after exporting the model.

## Startup lessons he shared
- Fell into most first-time-founder traps: hired fast on small cash, put all eggs in one basket, did not understand marketing, did not understand how to sell to architects (used to sell projects/vision, now needed to sell a product for money now).
- Spent far too long on big firms that take a year to decide; sales cycles range from ~3 months to a year. Says they were effectively broke about three times but recovered because they are independent.
- Nearly fundraised a year ago, declined last minute. Philosophy: product-first, small "family business" team rather than a 100-person pyramid. Investors told them the team matters most, then product.
- Founder-mode view: founders must stay hands-on in sales, marketing, customer success so they can judge delegated work. He applies the same to AI agents: agents run many processes (outbound, sales, assistant, marketing, coding) but humans decide. Says models got really good only since about December 2025.
- Investor trend: last 2-3 years pitches needed "AI"; now AI is a commodity and the edge is robotics. He sees simple site robots (painting, drilling) but positions the company as design tech, not construction tech.

## Views on AI and architects
- "There will always be a need in society for someone to take the blame." A licensed human must review and stamp even if 99% is AI-generated. The open question is whether the human share is the last 1% or the last 30%.
- Could a company stamp instead of a person? No: companies are always represented by a human, and no sensible person stamps what they do not understand; buildings collapse and people die, so society is unlikely to allow it.
- Mass-produced housing could be 90% AI-created; prestige city-center buildings will stay architect-designed.
- Only architects can build AI that produces buildings; even a well-funded big tech entrant would take 3-5 years because "there are a thousand places to take it wrong."
- Tacit knowledge of senior architects gets encoded as algorithms in the codebase.

## Interviewer questions worth keeping
- What did teams use before to check compliance? (Brief from client + national code + site rules, read manually.)
- Will AI take over design? Can a firm-level stamp replace individual architects? (Answered above.)
