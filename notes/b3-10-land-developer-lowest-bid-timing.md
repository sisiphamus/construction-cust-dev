# Developer C (Houston residential land developer) - lowest bid, timing is the biggest pain

## Deal cycle
- **Land first.** Location plays a huge role; a good school district is the biggest draw. Then physical characteristics: zoning where relevant, flooding and hurricane exposure, topography. Then physical/political checks, then a financial model. Investors look for a set IRR or equity multiple; if achievable, they put in an LOI and start feasibility.
- **Feasibility:** talk to engineers, MUD attorneys, land planners to firm up costs; turn that into a working budget until feasibility ends.
- **Site work:** detention ponds, raise the site (hauling dirt off costs money), utilities (pipe and electrical, then cover), curb and cover, dry utilities, fine grading, notice of substantial completion, then lots go to homebuilders. Mid-feasibility they engage homebuilders and set up contracts.

## Selecting homebuilders
- It depends on finding the right synergy in a community; a few builders to avoid at all costs; look at the top 10 and make a good guess. Judge product quality and performance in other communities. This is "a people business."
- Concern: a large builder can cut prices and wreck the others' margins, so only that builder sells, which is bad for the neighborhood; other builders ask the developer to intervene, though it is mostly out of the developer's hands.
- Ideal is ~3 builders per community, not 4; at 7-8 builders it becomes a "knife fight" and the builders blame the developer.
- Works primarily with a handful of the top Houston production homebuilders and has a particularly good relationship with one.
- Why homebuilders do not self-develop: they are publicly traded and self-development means lots of red on the books for years; the developer specializes in buying land they do not want on their balance sheet.
- Construction crews often do not read the design guidelines; they pour so many foundations a week they forget.

## Portfolio and tools
Six projects at different stages (one with residents in section 1 and moving to section 2, one not yet paved, one without any dirt moved), all with different investor bases, timelines and returns. Everything tracked in spreadsheets (different ones for different purposes). He personally handles closings and sends paperwork to the title company.

## Cost estimating and bidding
Estimates come from sending the work out to bid: GCs bid, utility contractors bid. After contract signing, price with GCs is typically fixed with occasional change orders. "We go for the lowest bidder, never seen it not be the lowest." Strong long relationships with subs who always bid lowest to stay on the projects, then "come back later and add change orders to increase their costs; some companies do that."

## Pains
- **Timing is the biggest issue.** Investors want a minimum low-to-mid-20s% IRR over ~5 years in and out; "time is the only asset money can't buy." Delays cost interest, delay returning money to investors, and there is an off chance a homebuilder drops its contract. The team is on site constantly to prevent unnecessary delays.
- Weather delays are the main cause. Example: a paver finished, left for other sites, and when county inspection required redoing the pavement it took weeks to get him back. Not frequent.
- Labor shortages ("absolutely"). Fuel costs are being passed through in bids.
