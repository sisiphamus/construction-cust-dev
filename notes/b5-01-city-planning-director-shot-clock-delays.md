# City Planning Director A (Texas suburban planning department) - delays come from the development side

## Summary
Customer-discovery interview (Interviewer 1) with the Director of Planning of a fast-growing Texas city (about 22,000 people when he joined 28 years ago; far larger now). Interviewers are Rice students studying city growth who had already met developers in Dallas, Austin and Houston, and wanted to compare permitting across cities.

Core frame: time and money are what matter to the developer. The interest clock starts when they buy land, so fast approval is the whole game. Zoning gets the most care because everything starts there, and the city is usually the party waiting on the development community to answer comments. He tells a developer upfront when the city will not support a project, and built a reputation for standing behind good projects through public hearings.

**Shot clocks and resubmittals.** Zoning cases and special use permits run long because of public hearings. Plans and plats are on a 30-day shot clock. A 2019 state law (he opposed it then, loves it now) ended year-long projects where some consultants gamed resubmittals as a cash cow, charging the client ~$10k per round without addressing all comments. In one case the developer fired the consultant after a joint meeting showed what was happening. He also sent every consultant and developer a letter: incomplete applications are flagged by email within 24 hours and not accepted until the deficiencies listed are fixed. This was triggered by seeing two staff "like hamsters in a wheel" emailing applicants about missing items every Monday-noon submission day; it pushed QC back onto consultants and "helped immensely".

**Where delays come from.** Their records show it is almost always the development side. Common applicant misses are petty: missing tax certificates, missing land authorization form (the city will not process a case where the applicant does not yet own the land without owner authorization; he cited a nearby city where a property was rezoned by someone who never closed, and the real owner later found it rezoned from commercial to single-family), plans that do not show divided roadways or median openings, wrong drawing scale. Small projects need more hand-holding than big ones because small developers lowball and hire cheap, fixed-fee consultants who do not know city standards, argue more, and lose money on extra rounds. Three projects are currently delayed 2-3 months because someone made field changes without review and penetrated a firewall, which also endangers tenants.

**Inter-department behavior.** Other departments historically said "it's not our job to help them design something". He instructs staff to be part of the solution; relationship building with consultants matters. Staff turnover on both public and private sides means constantly re-educating new people. He does not enforce deed restrictions (civil matter, not approved by council), and wants ordinances short, clear, with exhibits, and reviewed by code enforcement for clarity.

**Growth and standards.** The comprehensive plan has been updated four times; the city implements 70-80% of it. Raising standards did not kill development (a consultant's line: developers will build here with "money falling out of their pockets"). Three things make a city desirable: good schools, services (food, retail, city services), and aesthetics (distinct districts, not build-and-repeat). Change needs "flag bearers", not one planner alone. Some developers jokingly called his flurry of rewrites "ordinance of the week".

**Electronic plan review.** In place 6-7 years; removed people queuing 2-3 hours to submit. The city ran an RFP, hired a consultant to package requirements, narrowed to two finalists, and told the team to hire the best firm that can do the job, not merely the better of two (go back out if neither works). Annual surveys show a mix of people who love and hate the process, which he reads as "in the middle, good". Their system works better than Dallas's similar one because they trained staff on it ("Dallas handed staff a program and said go"). A weak manager on the planning side had to be corrected afterward. Each system change triggers a review of the whole document (FedEx analogy: a change at step 3 hits steps 10 and 15). Still gets complaints "here and there".

**Growth context.** Growth came faster than anyone realized, "like drinking from a fire hose", because the city sits on two major corridors. Hardest part is finding people who can do the work; he wanted the department to be a training ground for good planners, even if that means losing staff.

## Product-relevant takeaways
- Incomplete-application detection is already a formal city process (24-hour flag letter); a tool could do the same for applicants pre-submission.
- Resubmittal gaming is a named problem; shot-clock data and comment-response tracking are product angles.
- Software alone does not fix process (same type of system, different results depending on staff training).
- His view that delays almost always originate on the development side is a strong anchor for datasets.
