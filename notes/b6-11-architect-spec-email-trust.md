# Architect B (~250-person A/E firm) - spec management and email-automation trust

Interviewers 1 and 2 spoke with him; Interviewer 2 spoke briefly. Speaker attribution in the recording is partly uncertain.

## Workflow diversity
"The challenge with a lot of these tools is you have to be dynamic... it has to work for a 12-person firm and my 250 and somebody else's 2,500-person firm. You don't do residential projects the same way you do a hospital." Public schools have their own guidelines, vendors and standards, unlike private schools. Asked which firm type to target, he said that as non-architects it is "a whole career worth of things I'd have to explain." A CEO elsewhere wished for a tool that digs up past emails and carries context from previous projects into the next.

## Specs
- Master Excel database of spec sections covering everything from wall construction to stone type. For a project they pull sections (e.g., carpet), insert each manufacturer's different installation guidelines, and weave them section by section.
- Spec book ~1,000 pages for a moderate project, ~10,000 for a building the size of the conference venue. Any tool must work for a 12-person firm in Word, a 250-person firm with a cataloged library, and a 2,500-person firm with 100+ years of archives. It "took me months to understand what specifications were."

## Email
- ~100 emails/day per project. They need to separate follow-up items, FYI, and immediate/hot. Setup is repetitive at the start of every project.
- Tried an Outlook plugin: "give me the 10 hottest outstanding items." It missed important ones, e.g., a manufacturer saying a specified door is discontinued while the doors were due on site the next week. Missing that costs a lot of money.
- "Trust is slow to earn and fast to lose." Senior architects drop a tool the first time it fails.

## Signal
No buy signal; strong validation that spec cross-referencing is hours of manual work and that email-triage trust is the primary barrier to AI adoption in A/E firms.
