# Architect A (small Austin architecture firm) - how strong firms get permits through

## Summary
Research interview (permitting-focused) with the principal of a small Austin architecture firm. The interviewers were also contacting permitting offices in Houston, Harris County, Sugar Land, Galveston and other cities along the Houston-Dallas corridor. Architect A described the tactics that separate firms that clear permits quickly: pre-gaming with reviewers, pay-to-play lanes, and expeditors. He explained why multi-family stretches out, how Austin's resubmittal fees create bad incentives, and why the state accessibility variance process is broken. He expects AI plan review to arrive but to make things harder before easier.

## Decisions
None. Research interview; nothing owed either way.

## Key points

**Pay-to-play lanes**
- Many cities offer a dedicated paid lane with an expedited team. Instead of the plan circulating desk to desk, the whole review team sits in a room with the submitter and resolves issues live, versus a full round of comments, a scramble with all subs, and a resubmittal.

**Pre-gaming reviewers (his main advice)**
- "As much pre-gaming as you can do": contact specific reviewers, ask questions, and get answers before you submit; then you are "sailing."
- Works best in small jurisdictions with a handful of reviewers. Example: a project in a small Texas suburb with a single reviewer; they sent plans and questions first, the reviewer said which way it would go, they revised, and passed on the first submittal.
- Code-interpretation example: a small restaurant right at the 24/25 occupant line (two restrooms required at 25+). Occupancy can be computed by square footage or by seating plan; with 22 seats, requiring a second restroom would eat enough floor area to change the numbers. He took the calculations to the local authority before submittal and they accepted one restroom. The reviewer could have gone either way.
- In Austin it used to be possible to set up informal pre-conferences and earmark your submittal to that reviewer. That is gone; everything is online now.

**After round one**
- First-round comments are not blind. Each discipline has a named reviewer with email and phone. Practice: do not blindly resubmit. Send the reviewer your responses, ask them to confirm every comment is addressed, then resubmit. It is extra work but saves the client time, which is money.
- Some jurisdictions raise new items in later rounds that were not in the first; getting commitments by email removes the reason for a third round.
- In Austin the first resubmittal is free and later ones are charged; he suspects cities look for a third round because it increases revenue.
- He said no jurisdiction he has worked in issues blind comments, and not reaching out would be "a professional disservice."

**Expeditors**
- For jurisdictions he does not know (for example Chicago) firms use local expeditors, who add a communication step but act as a pre-review: they know the red flags and city relationships. In New York he says you absolutely need an expeditor because the process is relationship-based (every inspector needs to know the person meeting them). Chicago was described (by the interviewers) as requiring an architect or expeditor to submit, not the owner or GC.
- Relationship-driven permitting ("your plans were known by the reviewer") is largely gone.

**Why multi-family takes 3-4 rounds or more than a year (100-200 unit projects)**
- Communication gaps; affordable-housing components that put you in a different lane and require negotiated benefits and more consultants; more subs to coordinate.

**State accessibility variance (TDLR)**
- Example: an accessible roof deck with no elevator to the roof, but the same use is available on a lower, elevator-served floor. The variance had to be mailed in as a paper application; there is no portal; the only response is a letter acknowledging receipt, and there is no way to talk to anyone. It holds up the design and the city submittal because the city drawings cannot show the roof component until the state approves.

**AI plan review**
- No one has raised AI plan review with him before. He expects it to be "a whole lot harder before it gets easier" because models pull from institutional data and web information, much of which is wrong, and cannot yet tell which past responses were right or wrong.
- Human expertise, experience and taste will be worth more than automation.

**Client retention and differentiation**
- Once they win a client they rarely lose them; owners hear permitting and expediting horror stories and value the firm's ability to communicate with the permit office and get through quickly. Core takeaway: if you do not know the answer before the next round, you hand control of your fate to the city.
