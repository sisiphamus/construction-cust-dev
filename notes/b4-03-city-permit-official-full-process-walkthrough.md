# City Permitting Official A (large Texas city permitting center) - full permit process walkthrough and the missing feasibility gate

Interviewers: Interviewer 1 and Interviewer 2, visiting in person. The original was a very long transcript; this is the structured digest. A high-value insider for a permitting product.

## Who
Senior official who came up through the field, was a plan reviewer, ran the plan reviewers' office, technically retired about 5 years ago but remains in a senior role. Runs the city's pre-development meetings; bilingual, personally conducts meetings in Spanish for foreign developers (for example from Mexico and Colombia).

## The full high-rise path
1. **Replat + Planning Commission (~5-6 months).** Developers often buy land made of multiple recorded tracts and must combine them (replat) through the planning department. A replat triggers public notice and a Planning Commission hearing. This is essentially the ONLY point where the public can object to a project in a city with no zoning, and even then the commission only considers removing lot lines, not what is built. High rises and gas stations draw pushback. Plan on ~6 months with a city planner and civil engineers.
2. **Concurrent reviews (start before/with drawing submittal).** Water: apply for a letter confirming infrastructure capacity AND available capacity for the new units. Traffic: submit an access application; trips in/out are assessed, usually triggering a traffic study. These run parallel to the main submittal.
3. **Plan review (~15 business days per cycle, ~4 cycles, ~6 months).** Standard is 15 business days regardless of size (a doctor's office and a 30-story tower get the same clock). Expect ~4 cycles, ~60 business days, roughly 6 months. Intake routes the project to every department that must review: electrical, mechanical, plumbing, fire marshal, water, structural, signs, etc., up to 13-15 per project. A dashboard count of ~2,000 projects really means ~10x that many individual reviews. The city uses SPECIALIZED reviewers per trade (many from the field), unlike the single "4-hat" reviewer/inspector model in smaller jurisdictions.
4. **Fees.** Plan review is billed as a 25% deposit up front, with 75% due on approval. It is a deposit, charged once even if rejected repeatedly.
5. **Contractor registration.** GC, electrician, plumber and mechanical contractors must all be registered with the city.
6. **Inspection phase + phasing.** Projects can be split into multiple permits: horizontal/sitework (underground utilities, paving), foundation/piers (10-50 ft deep) separately, then superstructure; subterranean parking can be foundation to grade, then superstructure. Phasing keeps the field on schedule, but the risk is GCs calling inspections on the wrong project number. His advice for a high rise: open the FEWEST project numbers you can. Inspections are NEXT-DAY: schedule before 2:30pm and the inspector is there tomorrow.
7. **CO / TCO.** A Certificate of Occupancy is required to occupy (a "driver's license for the building"; many operate without one until caught). Developers often want to occupy before completion, which needs a Temporary CO, requiring exiting elements in place: stairs, shafts, fire protection. The goal is being able to "get the rabbit out of the building" before releasing floors.

## Why the city is fast (the edge)
- **Electronic plan review** (a commercial document-review platform) since roughly pre-COVID. Files live in the system, so they never missed a beat in COVID. He ran a controlled launch and asked architect friends to try to break it; the very first online permit was a cell tower for an out-of-state filer who found the not-yet-advertised system.
- **Pre-development meetings.** Offered here; New York and Chicago do NOT offer them. He runs them "selfishly": educating the developer before they submit makes both sides faster. He triages by the questions asked, pulls only relevant departments, or routes a simple question to the right person instead of making them wait two weeks for a formal meeting.
- **Speed vs peers.** New York permits per borough and inspections take 1-2 weeks. A California team faced 6-8 months for a traffic variance that this city turns in 4-6 weeks. The city has historically led the country in single-family permits.

## Local specifics ("backdoor zoning")
- **No zoning**, but de facto "backdoor zoning" via specific permits. Example: a liquor store cannot sit within ~1,000 ft of a church or daycare, handled by a separate regulatory department.
- **Noise / hours.** No hours-of-operation ordinance, so work can legally run at 2am, but decibel limits apply. Monolithic foundation pours happen at night (~300 concrete trucks staging on the road), generating noise/light complaints to the 311 line. Events exceeding decibel limits need a noise permit.
- **Flood.** Review runs through FEMA rules, rigid with little discretion (for example, a fixed elevation). A 100/500-year floodplain is a frequent deal-breaker; a floodway is essentially un-buildable; bringing in fill requires mitigation.

## What causes long delays
- **Public/political opposition (rare locally).** One high-rise near a university was locked ~2 years by public and political pressure; a new developer later bought the permit and restarted reviews. In another case neighbors flagged a long-gone cemetery on the tract; it was confirmed, the developer replatted around the graves and went back to the commission, and neighbors still fought it.
- **Funding drying up.** Developer 1 runs out of money, Developer 2 buys the permit and continues.
- **Permit validity.** A permit is good for 180 days; planning can extend once for another 180. In the field each inspection auto-extends it 180 days from the inspection date.
- **A failed inspection does NOT bounce you to plan review.** Only a revision for that specific element does. Stamped plans are legal documents, archived ~5 years.
- **Big projects are just long.** Hospital projects in the medical center run 2+ years because of equipment assessment. Event-related (World Cup) work is active now.

## Self-certification
Architects self-certifying code compliance: not allowed for foundations (the reviewer cannot see the rebar). San Antonio has a limited program for some residential projects and certain elements. He was skeptical of the city sending a large delegation to study it ("milkshake drive-through window": no separate fast lane for one small review). New York requires a pre-construction meeting with the building department before inspections can be scheduled; this city lacks the resources.

## Market read (mid-2026)
Construction is slow: interest rates, political climate and a war are making developers hold funds. Residential is way down: ~275 houses in the system versus a peak above 1,000; overall project counts have ranged up to ~2,000-4,000. Solar is the growth area, with a dedicated review team.

## Pre-development vs due-diligence meetings
- **Pre-development:** developer is committed and scouting locations (example: a coffee chain taking over former fast-casual sites that already have a grease trap/interceptor).
- **Due-diligence:** lots of what-ifs, project may not happen. He still meets them to surface deal-breakers early: floodplain; water capacity (you may think you can tie in on your own frontage but have to bore down another street, blowing the budget); and sign restrictions (a drive-through restaurant next to a competitor focused on sign count and height, with an overpass blocking the sign at allowed height).

## Staffing, pay, and why people leave
- "People leave managers, not organizations." In the city you inherit a team and cannot pick your people. Old-timers stay to retirement; younger people use the city as a training stepping stone. Consulting firms regularly try to recruit him.
- **Pay gap is large.** A city plan reviewer starting ~$70k can do the same job for a third-party reviewer/consultant starting ~$107k.
- **No real training program; knowledge moves by osmosis.** New reviewers sit next to old-timers with the code books open (he keeps the 2021 code, the 1994 UBC, even a 1972 code on his desk for cross-reference). New plan examiners shadow an inspector for a few days; new inspectors rotate through plan review. Schools do not teach building code. Where a review starts depends on discipline: structural at the site plan, electrical at the one-line diagram/panel schedule, mechanical at air changes/thermostats.
- **Pressure is from production volume, not life safety,** and flows down politically: complaints reach the mayor's office and come back down ("why has this been six months" when he saw it yesterday). People leave for lower-stress design-side jobs (one reviewer with a PhD left for a design team and said she could not believe how much pressure she had been under). Active crunch now on event-related and stadium projects.

## Longest / hardest reviews
Structural is the most time-consuming review. On the civil side, traffic takes longest. He splits the office into the "protein" teams (electrical, mechanical, plumbing) and the civil teams (detention, traffic studies, right-of-way).

## Traffic as a deal-breaker
Traffic approval can force a turn lane (left or right), often not in the budget, which can kill a project. A restaurant that generates more trips can trigger one. He has seen projects killed because an operator will not fund city infrastructure it cannot recoup if it closes in a few years.

## Airport tiers (a hidden deal-breaker)
The city has three airports, each with three concentric noise tiers. Tier requirements add sound attenuation (specific windows, doors, walls). Real example: a developer spent ~$30-50k on drawings and ~6-8 months in planning for an apartment complex, only to learn at structural review that the site was in Tier 1 where apartments cannot be built. Nobody told him earlier.

## No early feasibility gate (KEY for the product)
Interviewers pushed on this: platting, airport-tier checks, flood, water capacity and structural review happen in parallel or late, so a developer can burn months and tens of thousands of dollars before learning the site cannot work. The official agreed: "everybody dropped the ball, never told this guy." There is no upfront feasibility gate. This is a concrete, repeated pain and a direct opening for a fast upfront feasibility/eligibility check.

## Multifamily performance standard review
Apartments trigger a "performance standard review" involving the fire marshal, who checks fire access. The city uses fire access roads (so the largest apparatus can set up) rather than painted red fire lanes.

## Why this city is faster than small fast-growing towns
Interviewers noted developers along the Houston-to-Dallas corridor hit permitting/inspection delays of months to years in small towns; one large developer was losing ~$100k/day during a two-week stretch. His read:
- Volume-driven efficiency: more reps and years of trial and error.
- Separate specialized teams for review vs inspection. Small towns often use one "4-hat" person who does both; he doubts one person can hold that depth across trades.
- Next-day and after-hours/overtime inspections (for example a 2am foundation pour) that smaller cities do not offer.
- He thinks delays in other cities lean more to the INSPECTION side than review.
- Small-city quirk: a tree-protection ordinance in one small city can freeze a project until resolved.
- Inspectors are assigned to fixed zones (like zip codes), the same electrical/mechanical/plumbing inspector per zone, so they know active projects nearby. City cars carry GPS trackers.

## City Engineer's office (separate concurrent review)
Connecting to right-of-way (water/sewer connection, plan and profile) goes to a separate city engineer's office on its own timeline, concurrent with building-code review: another fragmented entity to satisfy.

## County vs city + a referral
The county is "a whole different animal." Historically only fire-code review plus civil, no building code (the fire code is essentially a maintenance code). The county has added oversight recently but is still lighter. He said it is easier to build a daycare in the county, but he would not put his own kids in one; the city's many reviews mean many eyes, which he equates to safety. **Referral: he offered to introduce the interviewers to the county chief engineer (a former acting director at the city).** Accepted; follow up by email.

## Design phase and architects want code pre-review
High-rise design takes 1-2 years before the city sees drawings, so the 15-day review is a small slice. Key signal: because architecture programs do NOT teach building code, designers actively want a third party to review drawings against code before they stamp them. Texas law also requires schools to be reviewed by a municipality that enforces building code (a rural school with no local oversight cannot be accredited without one). This is a demand signal for an AI code-check aimed at architects/designers, not just developers.

## Misc
- "The building outlasts the review": review time is crumbs versus a building standing 50-100 years. A themed aquarium restaurant project took ~8-10 months to permit (jungle finishes triggered flame-spread/smoke-index checks, live animals required zoo qualification, a rooftop helipad drew FAA objections).
