# Architects A and B (Southern California firm) - jurisdiction-specific code interpretation as moat

Participants: Interviewer 1, an architect, and the firm's business lead (Architecture Firm Business Lead A). About 11 minutes.

## Jurisdiction variability
- The state AIA chapter pushes consistency, but codes are interpreted differently per jurisdiction.
- One Southern California city is exceptionally strict and needs its own detail sets; New York City also needs different detail sets. Houston: reviewers claim "by the book" but enforcement is inconsistent and subjective. Miami is a major frustration source.
- Codes are often vague, leaving discretion to building officials.

## Firm workflow and moat
- Separate detail sets per difficult jurisdiction; new staff trained by tradition, not checklist. Applying overly conservative details everywhere prices architects out (contractors tell owners about the unnecessary cost).
- Relationship-based strategy: meetings with building officials to challenge interpretations, though officials have the final say.
- Client lock-in: a client in the strict city depends on the firm to navigate; the firm knows which reviewers are "good or bad." The firm deliberately does not share full knowledge with newer or out-of-region architects, to keep the moat. Sharing jurisdictional data would help the industry but erode competitive advantage; the moat is eroding as knowledge commoditizes.
- Some developer clients are "very detail oriented, very needy" and would consume all resources, so the firm turns down projects to protect service to good clients. A developer hiring architects in other regions without local knowledge pushes dependency back to the firm.

## Data concept (Interviewer 1)
- Problem example: a Houston developer had a reviewer demand a yellow building though the reviewer was not the owner; no visibility into reviewer behavior.
- Idea: aggregate data from developers, municipalities and reviewers to map average time to acceptance per reviewer, codes red-tagged most by each office, and timeline predictions. Value: clients see realistic timelines (8 months vs 3) backed by data; pitch "two times faster approval" with evidence. Interviewer 1: "It's a matter of data. If you can aggregate enough data for each reviewer, you'll have greater transparency."
