# Architect A (Austin multi-family architecture firm) - the site plan, not the building permit, is the bottleneck

Interviewer (a university entrepreneurship student) interviewing by phone, about 13 minutes; the architect was driving.

## Key points
- The firm does more work in Austin than anywhere else. "Permitting is always a hassle and a huge part of the schedule, especially in Austin." Other cities may take 2-4 months; for a new apartment project in Austin, "it's almost a guarantee to be at least a year."
- The biggest reason is the site plan review / site development permit, mostly civil engineer work with some architectural sheets included. The building permit can be approved, but needs the site plan approved first. The architect, mechanical, electrical, plumbing and structural side can do their portion in 3-4 months; the site permit is what takes ~12 months.
- Alternative review route: instead of the standard process (city gets six weeks to comment, you resubmit, city gets another six weeks), submit initial plans, the city takes about four weeks and gives basic comments, then a meeting with roughly 12 people on the call (architect, sometimes landscape architect, others) resolves things.
- Too many entities must sign off and "don't really talk": utility companies, right-of-way, transportation, the city water utility. Even a permit for an existing driveway can take a couple of months (city plan reviewers, then an analysis study, then transportation).
- Late surprises restart the clock. Halfway through site review, the water utility may say the water line must be upsized, which means trenching; known from the start it would be no big deal, but now they must resubmit to the first entity for re-approval.
- Legal: easements and license agreements with the city often cause extra delays. Depending on zoning the city requires trees along the right of way; that requires a license agreement, a separate legal process.
- The civil engineer runs point and the city development services department routes approvals entity to entity. The architect mostly just sends civil basic floor plans and building information and stays out of site review. He does not use the same civil engineer on every project.
- Several projects over the last 1.5-2 years were killed well into design when site work (such as water upsizing) surfaced. Why not found earlier: sometimes they do not know until an entity responds after submission; sometimes the developer should have known; sometimes code or ordinance changes land before submission so the project is not grandfathered. Major zoning/code changes are usually known ahead, but a few forced last-second pivots.
- Interviewer noted the AIA conference trend of site work becoming more and more expensive.
- Code tool: he likes Upcodes (met a founder, who visited the office for team feedback; the founders include a former architect). But he still keeps Austin's codes open in a tab, so code-lookup tools are not yet complete for local requirements.
