# Vendor A (Singapore data-center software) - construction-tech market and "advise, don't decide"

## Summary
Call between Interviewer 2 and Vendor A, who is based in Singapore and builds software that optimizes data-center cooling set points. Two threads: market intel on Singapore, and a product-liability principle.

## Key takeaways
- **Advise, don't decide.** Vendor A's software flags inefficiencies and recommends set points but does not auto-change them. Full autonomy would need an extremely robust (expensive) system, and it shifts liability to whoever turns it on (the vendor), pushing the price way up. Operators are not trained or prepared to take that on, and since most data-center downtime is human error, the prevailing mindset is never to touch running equipment. Interviewer 2 mapped this onto a mentor's advice for the permitting product: if you spot a plan out of code, or someone asks whether they can be 22 ft from the curb instead of 25 ft, do not fix it or answer yes/no; only recommend, so if wrong you "advised rather than did". Vendor A: "We can only advise... that's very good advice."
- **Singapore construction differs from Houston.** Mostly large condo developments (one permit can cover hundreds to ~1,000 units) plus government infrastructure; the private market is small with few deals, so contractors always bid low. Migrant labor from Southeast and wider Asia keeps costs down (now documented). Land is scarce and the population ageing, so the outlook is weak, which is why Vendor A moved from construction to software.
- **Why Singapore could still matter.** A Houston permitting product could plausibly be adapted (permitting has its own problems there). There is capital in Singapore seeking deployment, and it is a regional HQ hub for multinationals, so a natural place to sell, find a reseller, or raise money for US software entering Southeast Asia.
- **Data-center cooling business.** Data centers are designed for peak IT load but run far below it for the first years, wasting cooling energy. The software reads the building management system, trains AI to find set points for the current lower load, saving owners hundreds of thousands of dollars a year. Sweet spot is data centers ~4-6 years old (newer ones already efficient; older ones retrofit hardware). Adoption friction: mindset change and slightly higher operational risk, since operators must now actively manage set points with more protocols.
- **Interviewer pitch context.** Interviewer 2 described the Houston wedge: ~350,000 single-family permits a year, contractor trades crowded and bidding prices down, and an idea to reach homeowners (e.g. expecting families planning a house expansion, roof damage after floods) before competitors do, by analogy to how insurers reach injured people early. A Houston real-estate firm president said "permitting" ~90 times in an hour and offered to be the first customer for expediting software.
