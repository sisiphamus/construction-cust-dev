# City Plat Reviewer A (large Texas city planning department) - replat process walkthrough

Supply-side process detail; an office worker, not a buyer.

## When a replat is needed
- To put a use on land, subdivide it, or change land use. Lots restricted to single-family must be replatted to become commercial.

## Stage 1: check-in
- Applicant drafts and uploads everything on the portal, then submits during an open window (opens Friday 6 am, closes Monday 11 am, every other week).
- Required: existing survey, title report, the drawing, correct GIS coordinates, everything current.
- Reviewers check completeness after close. Missing or expired documents or unpaid fees produce an incomplete letter; the applicant has two cycles (about 4 weeks) or the application is inactivated. Fixing it Monday-Tuesday right after can still be accepted. Restarting costs only the already-paid fees.

## Stage 2: review
- A different planner takes over. Assignments go out Wednesday morning; reviews are due end of day the following Tuesday.
- Checks code compliance against the city's subdivision chapter, building lines, typos and details. Example: lots under the city's 3,500 sq ft minimum have options (50% building coverage, compensating open space, or an open-space amenities plan if in a floodplain).

## Stage 3: recordation
- Applicant books an appointment, pays fees, prints the final plat; a recordation planner confirms all markups were addressed. City signs and stamps; every Wednesday plats go to the county clerk (or county engineer outside city limits).
- Fast path: submit Monday, all clean, letter confirms check-in to the agenda; no further contact until recommended for approval at planning commission the following week (commission meets Thursdays).

## Failure modes
- Number one is unpaid fees (the client has not paid the applicant, or they forget). Then expired or missing documents, such as a drainage plan that takes a long time to get.
- Small and first-time applicants who do not know the process fail most.
- Multi-agency: county and other agencies leave comments that all must be cleared; one disapproval defers the project even if others approve.

## Reviewer workload
- About 12 projects per cycle, all due Wednesday-to-Tuesday. Most stressful: big cycles on a fixed clock and unresponsive applicants whose work rolls into the next cycle.

## Takeaway
- Confirms the pain is process navigation, document completeness and fee/coordination discipline for small or infrequent users. Aligns with a first-pass-approval or permitting-as-a-service wedge.
