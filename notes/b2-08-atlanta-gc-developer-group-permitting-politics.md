# Metro-Atlanta GC + developer group (permitting pain) - the real bottleneck is politics, not rules

## Participants (best-effort from content)

- Interviewer 1 and Interviewer 2 ran the call.
- Organizer A (assembled the group; not a main contributor).
- Commercial GC A (small-city and suburban metro-Atlanta work).
- Residential/Subdivision Builder A (single-family and large subdivisions in outlying counties).
- Commercial Developer A (grocery-anchored outparcel deals, rezonings, $30M+ projects): the most detailed and most software-skeptical voice.

## Key findings by theme

**Permitting has structurally slowed, especially post-COVID.**
- Commercial GC A: ~14 months to get land plus building permits on a 6,000 sq ft ground-up commercial job in a small Georgia city that has no online plan submission and outsources all review to a third-party engineering firm.
- The old playbook (a paid expediter who could walk a 3,000 sq ft tenant job to a permit in a day or two) is dead. Knowledgeable permit-office staff left during COVID; replacements lack experience and initiative. Even a simple small build-out is now 3-4 weeks minimum.

**Small municipalities are now as hard as, or harder than, big cities.**
- Organizer A: small cities "want to look and act like a big city but maintain small-town charm, so they over-regulate everybody."
- Residential Builder A: smaller outlying jurisdictions are getting stricter, partly reacting to neighbors upset by growth; erosion-control enforcement is sometimes used to nitpick or shut a site down. "The further south you go, the harder it gets."

**The real bottleneck is politics, not regulation (Commercial Developer A's throughline).**
- Public-notice and rezoning hearings are where projects die: anyone within ~1,000 ft of a rezoned parcel gets a letter and can show up to oppose. "No matter what you build, somebody says I don't want it."
- Election cycles are the wild card: a commissioner running for reelection will vote against a project for votes, even when it matches the city's own comprehensive land-use plan. His 32-acre commercial project fits the comp plan, yet the mayor warned of heavy opposition.
- Working countermeasures are relational and expensive: hire a land-use attorney and lobbyist (~$25K cited), rent a restaurant and run an informational session with color boards and a traffic study, and effectively placate the adjacent neighborhood (new patio furniture, redone landscaping and signage).

**Stormwater / erosion (NPDES) is a cost and shutdown lever.**
- On a 10-acre tract, site work cost ~$4.5M versus ~$1.8M land, driven by federal/state stormwater detention and water-quality requirements. "Land development costs blew past land costs two or three years ago."
- Rules are easy to comply with on paper but just as easy for an inspector to find any reason to shut a site down. Government exempts itself.

**The incumbent "solution" is a fixer; the developer doubts software can replace it.**
- "You hire [a known local fixer]... he walks in the back door, gets it stamped, and you pay him whatever you have to pay." He goes to one metro county "with a blank check."
- His blunt thesis: "The government does nothing well... there's really not a solution," and reviewers are people "who couldn't make it in the real world." He is the archetype of the AI-skeptical buyer who has made peace with pay-to-play.

## Implication

Software could address the guideline-vetting half (stormwater/NPDES, rezoning, third-party review rules are knowable), but not the people/politics half. This repeats a caveat raised by a Houston developer. The incumbent to displace is human expediters/fixers and pay-to-play.
