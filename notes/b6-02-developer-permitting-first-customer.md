# Developer A (Texas/Florida retail developer) - permitting is THE problem, offered to be first customer

## Bottom line
Developer A told the interviewers permitting is the problem and to build only an AI permit solution. He had been pitching the same idea to others himself and said "I'll be your first customer." Caveat he raised himself: much of the pain is people, not guidelines.

## Who he is
President of a family retail developer: convenience-service retail on 5-55 acre sites, 15-45k sqft centers anchored by mom-and-pop tenants. About 29 active projects in Texas and Florida, developed in 7 states over four decades, manages ~165 centers. Two sons work in the business on larger projects. Typical project size cited: ~$60-80M. Real buyer, not a tire-kicker. Referred in through a university real-estate network.

## The product he described
1. Ingest all permitting guidelines for a city, then the whole state.
2. Developer registers online, fills standard info, submits plans.
3. About 20 minutes later AI has vetted plans against every formal guideline and returns redlines; fix and resubmit; within a day you are AI-passed (phase 1).
4. Phase 2: a human at the city reviews practical items not in the guidelines (curb cuts, median cuts, traffic flow). Days to weeks instead of 6-24 months.
5. Charge a fee; separate residential vs commercial buckets for speed.
6. Centralize municipalities regionally so submitting to one city also flags ancillary items common across the metro.
7. Selling point: it eliminates nobody's job. A growing-suburb overlay idea (later session): young fast-growing towns lack mature codes and borrow from neighbors, so let them overlay their codes against mature neighbors without paying consultants.

## Why permitting, not cost or supply
- Timelines: 6-10 months typical; one tenant-pad project (fast-food plus coffee) was ~22-24 months in and still unpermitted. A multifamily project in another suburb had been stuck ~14 months with council meetings repeatedly postponed.
- Ripple effects: lost city tax revenue; lenders not earning interest on undrawn construction loans; equity-partner IRR curve flattens even though dollars do not change; tenant build-outs delayed, landlord eats rent and CAM.
- "Schedule slippage / material delays" raised by others is "a code word for permits."
- Cost: "Cost is going to be cost." During the lumber spike (~$600 to ~$1,800) they hedged by sequencing underground work first and saved roughly $750-800k, which on $60M projects "doesn't move the needle." No supply problems on five apartment projects built last year.
- He argued that a time-sensitive project is hurt most; larger, long-horizon players feel it less.

## Pain examples (the human half)
- Staff turnover and inspectors from other markets; reviews become personal; track record does not help; every municipality starts from scratch.
- Arbitrary asks not in any code: brick all the way around when the guideline said a percentage facing the main road; a ~$400k circular bus drive at an apartment project of one- and two-bedroom units (no families); building color preferences (paint a big shop to look like a taxi); 360-degree glass in one suburb versus stucco ends in another.
- Fire-department objection to 3 stories ("no truck that reaches the third floor"); he offered to buy a ~$250k truck and was refused; a different developer later got 3 stories because the city realized it was losing tax base to a neighbor.
- Permit fees: budgeted ~$150k across two small centers plus pads; the city assessed ~$250k per building plus ~$250k per out-parcel. A law firm could not find the formula; city said "we don't know until we issue a permit." Had they known, they would have platted as one building.
- Other permit layers: tenant build-out, sign, and landscaping permits. Some cities block tenant permits until the landlord gets a temporary or permanent C of O, and have been burned by developers skipping landscaping (fix: bond or escrow).
- Council agenda slots "not available this month," for months.
- Regions: coasts and Colorado are worse (Hawaii partner: 3-4 years; Colorado ~24 months for entitlements, rezoning "might as well call it 10 years"); Florida not bad; Nevada difficult (unions).

## Current workarounds
- Never go in cold: contact the municipality before submitting, set up face-to-face meetings; architects and engineers know the people. It still drags.
- Walk in with red pens and ask staff to mark up plans on the spot to avoid the 6-8 month loop; resubmissions still bounce for 2-3 months.
- Expediters: "window dressing, thousands of dollars, doesn't move the needle."
- Hiring a local attorney with community ties to push back on a council.
- Mayors and economic-development heads reply "we're looking into it."
- If he knew delay dates upfront he could time construction-loan drawdown to save months of interest (small fragment).

## Buy signals and next steps
- "I'm sold" / "go down that road" / "If things go well with the permitting office, maybe we can help you" / "I'll be your first customer."
- Forwarded a competing AI code-review platform and asked for the interviewers' read. Interviewer notes: it validates the wedge and the "preserve reviewer authority, kill no jobs" framing, but means they are not first; open questions are real quality on the guideline half, whether it touches the political half, and who its customers are.
- Offered architect introductions and invited the interviewers to his recurring mentor hours.
- Interviewer analysis: buyer is likely municipalities or architects rather than developers; developer is a champion/design partner.

## Follow-ups (2026-06-04 and 2026-06-10)
- Drafted (unsent) email by Interviewer 2 attaching a short demo video of a one-button plan upload-and-vet tool, mentioning that the head of the city's permitting office said they approve permits "at light speed" (contrast with the developer's experience), and asking whether stalled projects come from unforeseen issues or from known requirements dragging.
- At the 2026-06-10 cohort session the interviewers reported a week of ground research (visiting permitting and planning offices, a county office, and cities along a major Texas corridor), that Texas issues more than double the permits of the next state, that a code-tracking demo missed the real gap (the specific rules each municipality fixates on, hard to find without meetings and real case review), and that they were filing public-records requests for code data; one suburb answered "yes, please give us that information." Developer A: "data is the most powerful commodity right now."
- He observed that everyone the interviewers spoke to showed stress when permitting came up and listed cities they hate dealing with. "It's a space that is needed."
- Coaching theme to all student teams: think about distribution before customers; go to the large channel owners and chains, not door to door.
