# Architect A and Firm Business Lead A (Southern California architecture firm) - jurisdiction knowledge as moat, permitting data pitch

## Jurisdiction-specific interpretation
- A state architects association pushes consistency, but jurisdictions interpret codes differently. One strict Southern California city requires its own separate detail sets; New York City also needs different detail sets. Miami is a notable source of architect frustration.
- Houston: inconsistent enforcement; reviewers claim "by the book" but apply subjective interpretation. Codes are often vague, leaving discretion to building officials. Interviewer 2 cited a Houston developer whose reviewer demanded a building be yellow despite not being the owner, as an example of no transparency into reviewer psychology.

## Current workflow and moat
- The firm keeps separate detail sets per difficult jurisdiction; new employees learn by tradition, not checklist. Applying overly conservative details everywhere prices architects out (contractors tell owners about unnecessary costs).
- Relationship strategy: meet building officials to challenge interpretations, though officials have the final say.
- Client lock-in: a client in the strict city depends heavily on the firm, which knows which reviewers are good or bad. A developer hiring architects in other regions (e.g. Northern California) finds they lack local knowledge, pushing dependency back to the firm. The firm deliberately does not share full knowledge with newer or out-of-area architects, though it acknowledges this advantage erodes as knowledge commoditizes.
- Some developer clients are "very detail oriented, very needy" and would consume all resources; the firm turns down projects to maintain service to good clients.

## Data idea (Interviewer 2's pitch)
"We believe it's a matter of data. If you can aggregate enough data for each reviewer, you'll have greater transparency into the process." Aggregate developers, municipalities and reviewers to map average time to acceptance per reviewer, codes consistently red-tagged per office, and timeline predictions. Value: clients see realistic timelines (8 months versus 3) backed by data, and can pitch "two times faster approval" with evidence. A data approach could shift the moat from people to platform.
