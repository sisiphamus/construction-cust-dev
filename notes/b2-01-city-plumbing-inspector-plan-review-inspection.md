# City Plumbing Chief Inspector A (large Texas city permitting dept) - jobs fail because installs deviate from approved plans

## Context

The interviewers are building permitting software that vets plans against jurisdiction rules. This contact is a division manager and chief plumbing inspector, a direct line into how plan review and field inspection work and why submissions fail. A City Plan Analyst Supervisor A was present briefly. Treat as primary-source domain knowledge for the product and demo.

## Key takeaways

**Why jobs fail (the #1 thing): not installing per the approved plans.** Two recurring causes:

- Field value-engineering: contractors swap a spec'd material for a cheaper or preferred code-approved one (classic: engineer specs copper water piping, plumber installs PEX because it is cheaper and faster and comes in a roll). It looks fine but is not what the approved plans called for, so it is rejected.
- Bidding off the wrong drawings: plumbers bid off the construction set, not the approved set, so they are unaware another material was spec'd. Any deletion of fixtures or change of material requires resubmitting plans for a new approval. They skip it because resubmitting causes delays; the inspector says they never get a good answer on why.

**Codes and update cycle.** Each code body publishes about every 3 years. The city is on the 2021 code while 2024 and 2027 editions already exist, so it is behind and not moving to adopt soon. It went 2012 -> 2015 (held the old one for years), then 2015 -> 2021 within about a year.

- Two code bodies: ICC (I-codes, used by Dallas) and IAPMO (Uniform Plumbing/Mechanical Code, used by this city and likely Austin). Electrical is the separate National Electrical Code.
- Interpretation varies by region: soil (Houston digs easily; Austin/San Antonio hit caliche/rock), frost depth (water pipe minimum 12 inches deep in Houston, deeper in Dallas), and floodplain (in a known flood zone, appliances, gas meters and backflow preventers must be elevated).
- Code is a legal document: "shall" means must, "may" means discussable. Many code-compliant ways exist to build the same system as long as it meets the intent.
- Engineered alternates: when code does not address a material or method, the engineer supplies data, a separate group reviews it, and the building official signs off.

**Staffing and throughput.** Full staff would be 44 (permit techs, senior field inspectors, one assistant, inspectors). Chronically understaffed because city wages cannot match private (a union plumber vs city inspector can be ~$10/hr apart; private work runs 60-70 hrs/week plus on-call vs 40 here). A recent hire left after 4-5 months for private. Inspectors are in the field 90%+ of the time, averaging ~22-23 inspections/day (peak 30 with overtime); the target for a quality inspection is 15. Pre-COVID the office ran 650-700/day while better staffed. All inspections are cleared same-day. About 70% of inspectors are certified, which does not equal what they know. Hiring is via postings, social media and word of mouth; the main draw is stability and benefits.

**Red-tag education.** Most field plumbers do not own or read the code book. When a job fails, the inspector leaves a red tag listing deficiencies; that is how plumbers learn what to fix next time. Plumbers name-drop the chief ("he said I could do this") to pressure younger inspectors into passing. Standard: "I don't want to know what you think or feel, I want to know what you know" against the book. Takes 2-5 years to make a good inspector; turnover slows this.

**Inspector consistency.** Variation in leniency is personality, not experience: "Labrador" types want to be liked and pass things they should not; "new sheriff" types over-enforce on preference. The book is the standard.

**Why rules exist (life safety).** Cross-connection/backflow can contaminate potable water (garden hose in a fertilized puddle siphoned back during a main break; a kinked toilet fill tube pulling tank-tablet water into an ice maker; an improperly installed water softener in another Texas city pulling sewer water into drinking supply and forcing a boil notice). Hardest part of the job: argumentative contractors/customers who treat rules as suggestions.

**Tech and process changes.** 30 years ago everything was pen and paper; over ~20 years: handhelds -> phones -> tablets. Plans are now all-digital; inspectors and office both see them in the field. Recent mayoral initiative: residential plans out in 30 days. Residential gets lighter review (mostly architectural soundness), so the inspector field-verifies sizing, unlike commercial where drawings spell out pipe sizes.

**Complexity by job type.** Small jobs (lease spaces, outdoor kitchens, residential additions, apartments) are simple. Large ones (medical center, high/mid-rise, new hospital) get complicated: engineers miss overlays (plumbing runs into mechanical), beams unaccounted for, fast deadlines push contractors to request partial releases so they can occupy and get a draw ("it's always about the money"). Medical gas needs a separate Texas state endorsement and different code. Typical new-build inspection sequence: groundwork (all plumbing inside the footprint; grease and waste lines separate until the grease trap; trap primers on floor drains) -> rough-in (in-wall drainage, waste, venting, traps, fire caulking at penetrations) -> finals. Commercial coring of concrete floors is the most time-consuming part.

**Plan reviewers vs inspectors.** Specialized reviewers per trade. The chief inspector is the go-to liaison for plan review and for the occupancy group (which inspects already-occupied commercial sites for unpermitted alterations; every business needs a certificate of occupancy). Questions flow both ways to keep uniformity.

**Expediters / permit runners.** Some work closely with the office, especially engineers trying to pass plans first time, though plan review deals with them more than inspections. Others act as permit runners/middlemen chasing finals for contractors.

**Integrity.** Bribery is rare but real: this year one inspector was recorded soliciting a bribe; over ~30 years only a handful of cases. Random drug tests.

**Where to learn more.** The city permitting center website has the start-to-finish permit/submittal process and required documents. ICC and IAPMO codes and local amendments are online read-only. Nothing much beyond that for inspections; every city differs procedurally but shares a base code plus municipal amendments.
