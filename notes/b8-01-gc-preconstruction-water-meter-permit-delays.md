# GC Preconstruction Lead A (Houston multifamily GC) - permitting could be front-loaded; water meter is a hidden 6-8 week track

## Summary

Recorded at a Houston multifamily industry conference. Part 1 is a hallway conversation with a GC preconstruction/permitting lead, who was with a Project Manager and an Estimator from the same GC. The preconstruction lead's role: get work in the door to bid; the estimator makes sure it is bid properly; the PM makes sure it gets built. He says he mostly "puts fires out after" and that preconstruction is his main role.

His view: nearly everything could be handled on the front side, before the city approves the project. "Once they give that permit, there should be no more questions... once you get stamped drawings, there should be no more." The city does not require some items until later, which forces return trips and delay.

Part 2 is a stage talk on cost segregation (tax) and Part 3 an operator panel. Both are background, not about permitting.

## Key takeaways (permitting)

- **Water meter / water account is a separate, slow track.** Setup goes through "city legal," takes about 6-8 weeks, and is separate from the permit office and from city hall. It involves legal documents: deeds, metes and bounds, a survey. In multifamily the land owner is often not the apartment owner (ground lease), which adds paperwork.
- His argument: if the water meter is shown on the plans, all of that paperwork could be submitted and cleared before the job permit is issued. The city does not require it until you go to get the meter, so you are "almost closing the house" when you discover it, and you may have to leave and come back.
- **Fragmentation:** "every person we talk to mentions a new department." He had to track down about five different people. A long-tenured staffer (~15 years) in the permit office "knows" the process and ironed out a lot for him. He notes the city "doesn't really want you to come down there."
- Interviewer framing that landed: "You think you just pull a permit and you're good. Three months. You don't know." The path is described as "a roller coaster" of departments.
- Interviewers explained they are Rice students in a summer innovation program building software with a Houston developer to expedite permitting. The contact was friendly and wished them luck; no buy signal, no follow-up requested.
- The interviewers were mid-question about "what are you currently doing to get around this?" when the conversation ended, so workarounds were not captured.

## Cost segregation talk (background, not core)

A vendor presenting on cost segregation (an IRS-accepted depreciation method that puts short-life items into 5/7/15-year buckets instead of 27.5/39-year straight line, freeing cash early):
- Apartment complexes typically yield about 30% (range 30-40%) short-life items. Example: $1M depreciable basis yields ~$300K extra first-year depreciation and a bit over $100K first-year tax savings.
- 100% bonus depreciation is back for property acquired after mid-January 2025 (previously phased down: 80% in 2023, 60% in 2024, 40% early 2025).
- Older purchases can use a one-time catch-up study via a change-in-method-of-accounting filing.
- Typical study takes ~60 days; priced by location, size and complexity; the vendor offers a no-obligation preliminary analysis and audit defense; says it has done 15,000+ studies with a handful of IRS questions.

## Operator panel (background)

Panel of multifamily operators and developers on leasing, management and operations:
- Houston moves as submarkets. Supply is slowing; recovery expected submarket by submarket, likely late 2026 to early 2027, before stabilized occupancy (~93%) returns. Concession-driven market; stabilized properties at roughly 11-12% vacancy. Refinancing at rates about double the prior cycle. Workforce housing hit hardest. Execution gaps hidden in the 2021-22 run-up are now visible.
- Theme: customer service is the differentiator and has been underinvested; the "must-have amenity" is excellent service. Measure NOI, not just occupancy. Empower staff as owners; training, mentoring and engagement scores.
- Ideas: sit delinquent residents down for a financial review (one found a resident spending $1,000+/month on rideshare); early AI adoption to cut redundant tasks, with a caution that tech often adds screen time rather than freeing it; one operator leased 62 units with zero concessions by shifting the brand from concession-giver to experience-giver.
- Quote: "If you're putting a camera in the office to watch what they're doing, you're way lost."

## Relevance to the permitting thesis

Reinforces the recurring finding that permitting is a multi-department process with hidden steps that surface late. Specific, concrete candidate wedge: surface the water-account and legal-document steps early so they run in parallel with permit review.
